Mock Paper 1 — Applications of Mathematical Knowledge

Twenty multiple-choice questions in 75 minutes, no calculator. This is an original paper written for this site: the topic mix, the difficulty curve and the style of the wrong answers all follow the real TMUA Paper 1 specification.

Instructions — read before you start
  • Time: 75 minutes for 20 questions — an average of 3 minutes 45 seconds each, but the early questions should take far less, buying you time for the last five.
  • Most questions have five options; four have six. The real test varies the count too, and the longer lists mark the harder questions — Questions 11, 18, 19 and 20 are pitched at the level of the JZ Maths mocks, which are deliberately harder than the real TMUA. Do not be discouraged by them.
  • No calculator and no formula sheet. Answers on paper first; only then open the solutions.
  • One mark per question, no negative marking — so never leave a question blank. If you are down to two options, guess and move on.
  • Mark yourself against the answer key at the bottom, then read every solution, including for the questions you got right — the fast route is often not the one you took.

Section A — Questions 1 to 20

Question 1Indices & logarithms

Given that \(2^{x}=5\) and \(2^{y}=3\), what is \(\log_2\left(\dfrac{45}{8}\right)\) in terms of \(x\) and \(y\)?

  1. \(2x+y-3\)
  2. \(xy-3\)
  3. \(x+2y-3\)
  4. \(x+2y+3\)
  5. \(2(x+y)-3\)
Show solution

Answer: C

Read the hypotheses as logarithms: \(x=\log_2 5\) and \(y=\log_2 3\). Then \[\log_2\frac{45}{8}=\log_2\frac{5\cdot 3^2}{2^3}=\log_2 5+2\log_2 3-3=x+2y-3.\] The trap is A, which comes from factorising \(45\) as \(5^2\cdot 3\) instead of \(5\cdot 3^2\). Always factorise the number on paper before converting.

Question 2Quadratics

The equation \(x^2+(k+2)x+(2k+1)=0\) has equal roots. What is the sum of the possible values of \(k\)?

  1. \(0\)
  2. \(2\)
  3. \(4\)
  4. \(6\)
  5. \(-4\)
Show solution

Answer: C

Equal roots means the discriminant vanishes: \[(k+2)^2-4(2k+1)=k^2+4k+4-8k-4=k^2-4k=0,\] so \(k(k-4)=0\) and \(k=0\) or \(k=4\); the sum is \(4\).

Faster: you never needed the roots. The sum of the solutions of \(k^2-4k=0\) is \(-\frac{b}{a}=4\) by Vieta — worth a second's thought whenever a question asks for a sum of parameter values.

Question 3Polynomials

The cubic \(f(x)=x^3+ax^2+bx-6\) is divisible by both \((x-1)\) and \((x+2)\). What is its third root?

  1. \(3\)
  2. \(-1\)
  3. \(6\)
  4. \(-6\)
  5. \(-3\)
Show solution

Answer: E

Do not find \(a\) and \(b\). For a monic cubic \(x^3+ax^2+bx+c\) the product of the roots is \(-c\), here \(-(-6)=6\). Two of the roots are \(1\) and \(-2\), so if the third is \(r\), \[1\cdot(-2)\cdot r=6\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r=-3.\] (If you did solve for the coefficients you would get \(a=4\), \(b=1\), and \(x^3+4x^2+x-6=(x-1)(x+2)(x+3)\) — same answer, three times the work.)

Question 4Binomial theorem

What is the coefficient of \(x^2\) in the expansion of \((1+x)(1-2x)^5\)?

  1. \(40\)
  2. \(30\)
  3. \(50\)
  4. \(-30\)
  5. \(20\)
Show solution

Answer: B

Expand only as far as you need: \[(1-2x)^5=1-10x+40x^2-\cdots\] since \(\binom{5}{1}(-2)=-10\) and \(\binom{5}{2}(-2)^2=10\cdot 4=40\). An \(x^2\) term in the product comes either from \(1\times 40x^2\) or from \(x\times(-10x)\), giving \(40-10=30\).

Option A is what you get by forgetting the \((1+x)\) factor altogether — a very common slip when the bracket sits in front.

Question 5Sequences & series

A sequence \(a_1,a_2,a_3,\dots\) is such that \(a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n=2n^2-3n\) for every positive integer \(n\). What is \(a_{10}\)?

  1. \(37\)
  2. \(170\)
  3. \(17\)
  4. \(35\)
  5. \(33\)
Show solution

Answer: D

Notice first that you are told nothing about what kind of sequence this is — and you need nothing. Writing \(S_n\) for the sum of the first \(n\) terms, the given formula holds for every \(n\), and that alone pins down every term, through the single most useful identity about partial sums, \(a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\): \[a_{10}=S_{10}-S_9=(200-30)-(162-27)=170-135=35.\] Option B is \(S_{10}\) itself — read the question.

Having found the answer, you can check the structure out of interest: \(a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}=4n-5\) for \(n\ge 2\), and \(a_1=S_1=-1\) fits the same formula, so the sequence turns out to be arithmetic with common difference \(4\). A quadratic \(S_n\) always produces an arithmetic sequence — but here that is a consequence, not something you were given or needed.

Question 6Geometric series

A geometric series has sum to infinity \(27\), and the sum of its first two terms is \(15\). What is the sum of the possible values of its first term?

  1. \(9\)
  2. \(27\)
  3. \(45\)
  4. \(36\)
  5. \(54\)
Show solution

Answer: E

With first term \(a\) and ratio \(r\) (where \(|r|<1\)): \[\frac{a}{1-r}=27,\qquad a(1+r)=15.\] Substituting \(a=27(1-r)\) into the second gives \(27(1-r)(1+r)=15\), so \(1-r^2=\frac59\) and \(r=\pm\frac23\) — both admissible, since both have modulus less than \(1\).

Then \(a=27\left(1-\frac23\right)=9\) or \(a=27\left(1+\frac23\right)=45\), and the sum is \(54\).

The difference between a good and a great mark on this question is noticing the second root: \(r^2=\frac49\) has two solutions and nothing in the question rules either out. Options A and C are each half of the answer.

Question 7Circles

What is the length of the tangent from the point \((8,6)\) to the circle \(x^2+y^2-6x+4y-12=0\)?

  1. \(\sqrt{89}\)
  2. \(5\)
  3. \(8\)
  4. \(13\)
  5. \(10\)
Show solution

Answer: C

Complete the square: \((x-3)^2+(y+2)^2=12+9+4=25\), so the centre is \(C=(3,-2)\) and the radius is \(5\).

The tangent, the radius to the point of contact and the line to the centre form a right-angled triangle, so with \(d=|PC|\) the tangent length \(t\) satisfies \(t^2=d^2-r^2\): \[d^2=(8-3)^2+(6+2)^2=25+64=89,\qquad t=\sqrt{89-25}=\sqrt{64}=8.\] Option A is \(d\) itself; option B is the radius.

Question 8Lines and circles

The line \(y=2x+c\) is a tangent to the circle \(x^2+y^2=5\). What is the value of \(c^2\)?

  1. \(25\)
  2. \(5\)
  3. \(20\)
  4. \(45\)
  5. \(1\)
Show solution

Answer: A

Tangency means the perpendicular distance from the centre \((0,0)\) to \(2x-y+c=0\) equals the radius \(\sqrt5\): \[\frac{|c|}{\sqrt{2^2+(-1)^2}}=\sqrt5\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{|c|}{\sqrt5}=\sqrt5\ \Longrightarrow\ |c|=5.\] So \(c^2=25\).

The discriminant route, if you prefer it: substituting gives \(5x^2+4cx+c^2-5=0\), and \(16c^2-20(c^2-5)=0\) gives \(c^2=25\) again. The distance formula is quicker whenever the circle is centred conveniently — learn to spot which of the two is cheaper.

Question 9Trigonometric equations

What is the sum of all solutions of \(2\sin^2 x=3\cos x\) in the interval \(0\le x\le 2\pi\)?

  1. \(\pi\)
  2. \(2\pi\)
  3. \(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\)
  4. \(\tfrac{5\pi}{3}\)
  5. \(\tfrac{\pi}{3}\)
Show solution

Answer: B

Convert to a single function using \(\sin^2x=1-\cos^2x\): \[2(1-\cos^2x)=3\cos x\ \Longrightarrow\ 2\cos^2x+3\cos x-2=0\ \Longrightarrow\ (2\cos x-1)(\cos x+2)=0.\] The factor \(\cos x=-2\) is impossible, so \(\cos x=\frac12\), giving \(x=\frac{\pi}{3}\) and \(x=\frac{5\pi}{3}\) in the interval. Their sum is \(2\pi\).

Discarding the impossible root is the whole point of the question: the examiner wants to see whether you check that \(|\cos x|\le 1\) rather than reaching for a calculator that isn't there.

Question 10Trigonometry

What is the exact value of \(\sin 75^\circ\cos 15^\circ\)?

  1. \(\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\)
  2. \(\dfrac{2-\sqrt3}{4}\)
  3. \(\dfrac12\)
  4. \(\dfrac{2+\sqrt3}{4}\)
  5. \(\dfrac{1+\sqrt3}{4}\)
Show solution

Answer: D

Since \(\sin 75^\circ=\cos 15^\circ\), the product is \(\cos^2 15^\circ\), and by the double-angle formula \[\cos^2 15^\circ=\frac{1+\cos 30^\circ}{2}=\frac{1+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}{2}=\frac{2+\sqrt3}{4}.\] Option A is \(\cos 15^\circ\) itself (a tempting sight, since \(\left(\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\right)^2\) does equal the answer — squaring it is the slower route to the same place).

Sanity check: \(\cos 15^\circ\) is a little under \(1\), so its square should be a little under \(1\); \(\frac{2+\sqrt3}{4}\approx 0.93\). Good. A three-second numerical check like this catches most sign and factor errors.

Question 11Logarithms

The positive real numbers \(x\) and \(y\) satisfy \[\log_2\!\left(xy^2\right)=5,\qquad \left(\log_2 x\right)\left(\log_2 y\right)=2.\] What is the sum of all possible values of \(\log_2\!\left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right)\)?

  1. \(\tfrac52\)
  2. \(\tfrac72\)
  3. \(-1\)
  4. \(3\)
  5. \(\tfrac92\)
  6. \(5\)
Show solution

Answer: A

Substitute before you do anything else. Put \(a=\log_2 x\) and \(b=\log_2 y\); the logarithm laws turn both conditions into ordinary algebra: \[a+2b=5,\qquad ab=2,\] and the quantity wanted is simply \(\log_2(x/y)=a-b\).

Eliminating \(a=5-2b\) gives \((5-2b)b=2\), i.e. \(2b^2-5b+2=0\), so \((2b-1)(b-2)=0\) and \(b=2\) or \(b=\tfrac12\). The two solutions are \[(a,b)=(1,2)\quad\text{and}\quad (a,b)=\left(4,\tfrac12\right),\] both legitimate, since \(x=2^a\) and \(y=2^b\) are positive for any real \(a,b\) — there is nothing to reject.

Hence \(a-b=-1\) or \(a-b=\tfrac72\), and the sum is \(-1+\tfrac72=\tfrac52\).

Options C and B are the two individual values: whenever a question asks for the sum of all possible values, the examiner has already anticipated that you will find one root and stop. Solving a quadratic and using only one root is the most expensive habit on this paper.

Question 12Logarithms

Given that \(\log_{10}2=0.3010\) to four decimal places, how many digits does \(2^{100}\) have?

  1. \(31\)
  2. \(30\)
  3. \(60\)
  4. \(100\)
  5. \(32\)
Show solution

Answer: A

A positive integer \(N\) has \(\lfloor\log_{10}N\rfloor+1\) digits — because \(N\) has \(d\) digits exactly when \(10^{d-1}\le N<10^{d}\).

Here \(\log_{10}2^{100}=100\times 0.3010=30.10\), so \(\lfloor\log_{10}2^{100}\rfloor=30\) and \(2^{100}\) has \(31\) digits. (Indeed \(2^{100}\approx 1.27\times 10^{30}\).)

Option B is the classic off-by-one: the exponent counts the powers of ten, not the digits.

Question 13Differentiation

The curve \(y=x^3-3x^2-9x+5\) has a local maximum and a local minimum. What is the difference between the two corresponding \(y\)-values?

  1. \(12\)
  2. \(22\)
  3. \(10\)
  4. \(4\)
  5. \(32\)
Show solution

Answer: E

Differentiate: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=3x^2-6x-9=3(x-3)(x+1)\), so the stationary points are at \(x=-1\) and \(x=3\).

Substituting back: at \(x=-1\), \(y=-1-3+9+5=10\); at \(x=3\), \(y=27-27-27+5=-22\). Since the cubic has a positive leading coefficient, the left stationary point is the maximum. The difference is \(10-(-22)=32\).

Option A is \(x=3\) minus \(x=-1\) — the horizontal gap, not the vertical one. Option B forgets the sign of the minimum.

Question 14Tangents

Two tangents to the parabola \(y=x^2\) pass through the point \((0,-4)\). What is the distance between their two points of contact?

  1. \(2\)
  2. \(4\)
  3. \(8\)
  4. \(2\sqrt2\)
  5. \(4\sqrt2\)
Show solution

Answer: B

Work with a general point of contact \((a,a^2)\). Since \(y'=2x\), the tangent there is \[y-a^2=2a(x-a)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y=2ax-a^2.\] It passes through \((0,-4)\) precisely when \(-a^2=-4\), i.e. \(a=\pm2\).

The points of contact are therefore \((2,4)\) and \((-2,4)\) — at the same height, so the distance is simply \(4\).

Option E comes from computing \(\sqrt{4^2+4^2}\), i.e. forgetting that both contact points share the \(y\)-value \(4\). Sketching the parabola with its axis of symmetry makes this instant.

Question 15Areas

What is the area of the region enclosed between the curve \(y=x^2\) and the line \(y=2x+3\)?

  1. \(\tfrac{16}{3}\)
  2. \(8\)
  3. \(\tfrac{32}{3}\)
  4. \(\tfrac{64}{3}\)
  5. \(10\)
Show solution

Answer: C

They meet where \(x^2=2x+3\), i.e. \(x^2-2x-3=(x-3)(x+1)=0\), so \(x=-1\) and \(x=3\). Between them the line is above the parabola, so \[A=\int_{-1}^{3}\left(2x+3-x^2\right)dx=\left[x^2+3x-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^{3}=(9+9-9)-\left(1-3+\frac13\right)=9+\frac53=\frac{32}{3}.\]

Worth memorising: for any line meeting any parabola \(y=ax^2+\cdots\) at \(x=p\) and \(x=q\), the enclosed area is \(\frac{|a|(q-p)^3}{6}\). Here that is \(\frac{1\cdot 4^3}{6}=\frac{32}{3}\) — a five-second answer, and the formula is easy to derive once and reuse forever.

Question 16Integration

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_1^4\frac{\left(\sqrt x+1\right)^2}{x}\,dx\).

  1. \(7+\ln 2\)
  2. \(12+2\ln 2\)
  3. \(3+4\ln 2\)
  4. \(7+2\ln 2\)
  5. \(7\)
Show solution

Answer: D

Expand first — there is no integration technique to reach for here, only algebra: \[\frac{(\sqrt x+1)^2}{x}=\frac{x+2\sqrt x+1}{x}=1+\frac{2}{\sqrt x}+\frac1x.\] Hence \[\int_1^4\left(1+2x^{-1/2}+x^{-1}\right)dx=\Big[x+4\sqrt x+\ln x\Big]_1^4=(4+8+\ln 4)-(1+4+0)=7+2\ln 2,\] using \(\ln 4=2\ln 2\).

Option A keeps \(\ln 4\) unsimplified as \(\ln 2\) — check what the options are asking for before you stop simplifying.

Question 17Solids of revolution

The region enclosed between \(y=x\) and \(y=x^2\) for \(0\le x\le 1\) is rotated through \(2\pi\) about the \(x\)-axis. What is the volume of the solid formed?

  1. \(\tfrac{\pi}{15}\)
  2. \(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\)
  3. \(\tfrac{8\pi}{15}\)
  4. \(\tfrac{\pi}{30}\)
  5. \(\tfrac{2\pi}{15}\)
Show solution

Answer: E

Rotating a region between two curves gives a washer, so you subtract the squares, not the curves: \[V=\pi\int_0^1\left(x^2-\left(x^2\right)^2\right)dx=\pi\int_0^1\left(x^2-x^4\right)dx=\pi\left[\frac{x^3}{3}-\frac{x^5}{5}\right]_0^1=\pi\left(\frac13-\frac15\right)=\frac{2\pi}{15}.\]

Option B is \(\pi\int_0^1(x-x^2)^2dx\) — squaring the difference instead of differencing the squares. These are genuinely different, and the examiners know it.

Question 18Counting

How many four-digit numbers greater than \(4000\) can be formed using the digits \(1,2,3,4,5,6\), with no digit repeated, if the number must be even?

  1. \(60\)
  2. \(72\)
  3. \(90\)
  4. \(96\)
  5. \(108\)
  6. \(84\)
Show solution

Answer: F

Two constraints act on two different positions, and they interfere — so handle the pair of constrained positions together, and only then fill the rest.

The last digit must be even: \(d_4\in\{2,4,6\}\). The first digit must satisfy \(d_1\ge4\) (any such number automatically exceeds \(4000\), since the smallest is \(4123\)), so \(d_1\in\{4,5,6\}\). That looks like \(3\times3=9\) pairs, but \(d_1\ne d_4\), and the two sets share the digits \(4\) and \(6\) — so two pairs are illegal, leaving \(9-2=7\).

Whatever that pair is, the middle two positions are filled from the remaining \(4\) digits in \(4\times3=12\) ways. Total: \[7\times12=84.\]

The trap is option D. Treating the constraints as independent gives \(3\times3\times12=108\) (option E), and "fixing" it by subtracting a whole digit's worth of cases rather than exactly the two overlapping pairs gives \(96\). When two constraints compete for the same digits, count the constrained positions jointly; the alternative is a case split on whether \(d_4=4\) or \(6\) (then \(d_1\) has 2 choices) or \(d_4=2\) (then \(d_1\) has 3), giving \(2\times2\times12+1\times3\times12=48+36=84\). Same answer, and a good check.

Question 19Probability

Two fair six-sided dice are rolled repeatedly until the total is either \(7\) or \(8\), at which point the game stops. What is the probability that the game stops on a total of \(7\)?

  1. \(\tfrac12\)
  2. \(\tfrac{6}{11}\)
  3. \(\tfrac16\)
  4. \(\tfrac{5}{11}\)
  5. \(\tfrac{11}{36}\)
  6. \(\tfrac{6}{36}\)
Show solution

Answer: B

There are \(6\) ordered ways to total \(7\) and \(5\) ways to total \(8\), out of \(36\). Every roll that gives neither simply repeats the situation unchanged, so conditioning on the game ending on a given roll, the probability that it ended on a \(7\) is \[\frac{6/36}{6/36+5/36}=\frac{6}{11}.\]

Why you may ignore the waiting. Summing the series confirms it. With \(p=\frac{6}{36}\), \(q=\frac{5}{36}\) and \(r=1-p-q\) the probability of no decision on a roll, \[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r^n p=\frac{p}{1-r}=\frac{p}{p+q}=\frac{6}{11},\] so the geometric series collapses to exactly the ratio above. Recognising that structure — irrelevant outcomes rescale nothing — turns a page of algebra into one line, and it is the whole point of the question.

Option F is \(\frac{6}{36}\), the chance of a \(7\) on the first roll alone; option A assumes the two totals are equally likely, which they are not (\(7\) is the most likely total of two dice, \(8\) is not); option D is the answer to the complementary question.

Question 20Modulus graphs & areas

What is the area of the finite region enclosed between the curve \(y=\left|x^2-4\right|\) and the line \(y=5\)?

  1. \(\tfrac{28}{3}\)
  2. \(36\)
  3. \(\tfrac{16}{3}\)
  4. \(\tfrac{44}{3}\)
  5. \(\tfrac{52}{3}\)
  6. \(18\)
Show solution

Answer: D

Sketch it. The parabola \(y=x^2-4\) has its portion between \(x=-2\) and \(x=2\) reflected upwards, producing a "W" with zeros at \(x=\pm2\) and a local maximum of \(4\) at the origin. The line \(y=5\) sits above that hump, so the region is a single connected piece.

The boundaries meet where \(\left|x^2-4\right|=5\). Only \(x^2-4=5\) is possible (\(x^2-4=-5\) would need \(x^2=-1\)), so \(x=\pm3\).

Now split at \(x=\pm2\), where the modulus changes definition — this is the step the question is really testing: \[A=\int_{-3}^{3}\left(5-\left|x^2-4\right|\right)dx=2\left[\int_0^2\left(5-(4-x^2)\right)dx+\int_2^3\left(5-(x^2-4)\right)dx\right]\] by symmetry. The two pieces are \[\int_0^2\left(1+x^2\right)dx=2+\frac83=\frac{14}{3},\qquad \int_2^3\left(9-x^2\right)dx=\left[9x-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_2^3=18-\frac{46}{3}=\frac83,\] so \(A=2\left(\frac{14}{3}+\frac83\right)=\frac{44}{3}\).

Every wrong option is a specific failure to split. Option B, \(36\), ignores the modulus altogether and integrates \(5-(x^2-4)\) across the whole of \([-3,3]\) — the area under the unreflected parabola, which is exactly the parabola-and-line formula \(\frac{|a|(q-p)^3}{6}=\frac{6^3}{6}=36\) from Question 15. Option A, \(\frac{28}{3}\), keeps only the middle piece over \([-2,2]\); option C, \(\frac{16}{3}\), keeps only the two outer wings. The discipline is always the same: a modulus sign means the integral splits wherever the inside changes sign, and a sketch tells you where.

Estimate to check: the region sits inside the rectangle \(-3\le x\le3\), \(0\le y\le5\) of area \(30\), and clearly occupies about half of it. \(\frac{44}{3}\approx14.7\). Good.

Interpreting your score

On the real TMUA the raw mark is scaled to a score out of 9.0, and the scaling shifts year to year — so treat the bands below as rough guidance rather than a prediction.

  • 16–20: comfortably competitive for Cambridge and Imperial. Your remaining gains are in speed, not knowledge.
  • 12–15: a solid base. Sort your dropped marks into knowledge / technique / logic as described on the exam papers page and work the weakest topic.
  • 8–11: the mathematics is mostly there but not yet automatic. Go back through the topic pages and redo every question you missed from scratch a week later.
  • Below 8: don't be discouraged, and don't move on to more papers yet — papers diagnose, topics cure. Start with the topic that produced most of your errors.

When you have marked this paper, do Mock Paper 2 — the reasoning paper — under the same conditions, and then move on to the real past papers and the other free mock papers listed on the TMUA exam papers page.
