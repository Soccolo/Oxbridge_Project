Functions

Limits, asymptotes and the art of curve sketching — plus logarithms, graph transformations and functional equations.

§5.1Introduction

We are now ready to tackle one of the largest chapters of Mathematics: Calculus. Having defined numbers and sequences, we begin with the theory of functions, move on to limits, curve sketching and asymptotes, conclude the sketching discussion with geometric transformations, and finish with basic functional equations.

§5.2Basic definitions

Functions

A function is a mapping of numbers from a set \(X\) to a set \(Y\) (here, subsets of \(\mathbb R\)), written \(f:X\to Y\).

The elementary functions are:

  1. Constant functions: \(f(x)=a\).
  2. Rational powers of \(x\): \(f(x)=x^a\), \(a\in\mathbb Q\), e.g. \(x^{3/2}\).
  3. Exponential functions: \(f(x)=a^x\) with \(a>0\), e.g. \(2^x\).
  4. Logarithms: \(f(x)=\log_a(x)\) — the inverse of exponentiation: if \(a^b=x\) then \(\log_a(x)=b\). We write \(\ln(x)=\log_e(x)\).
  5. Trigonometric functions: \(\sin x,\ \cos x,\ \tan x,\dots\)
  6. Inverse trigonometric functions: \(\arcsin x,\ \arccos x,\ \arctan x,\dots\)
  7. All functions obtained by adding, subtracting, multiplying and composing the above.

Always think about \(X\) and \(Y\). Raising to an even power gives \(Y\subseteq\mathbb R_+\); a rational power with even denominator restricts \(X\) too. The quantity \(\log_a(b)\) needs \(a>0\) and \(b>0\). When solving an equation \(f(x)=a\) built from elementary functions, first set boundaries on the possible values of \(x\) (e.g. \(x>0\) if it sits inside a logarithm), and check whether \(a\) is attainable at all. Finding constraints for \(X\) and \(Y\) roots out false solutions and makes graphing easier.

§5.2.1 Logarithms

The logarithm feels abstract to many students: \(y=e^x\) is easy to picture; \(x=\ln y\) is easy to understand on the spot but hard to internalise. In the exam or interview you are expected to evaluate \(\log_a(b)\) very quickly, so internalise these laws:

Laws of logarithms
  1. \(\log_a(x)+\log_a(y)=\log_a(xy)\). To see it: \(a^{\log_a x}\cdot a^{\log_a y}=xy=a^{\log_a x+\log_a y}\); take \(\log_a\) of both sides.
  2. \(\log_a\!\left(x^k\right)=k\log_a(x)\), for any \(k\). (Replace \(y\) with \(x\) or \(x^{-1}\) repeatedly to get all integer \(k\); take for granted that it extends to non-integers.)
  3. \(\displaystyle\log_a(x)=\frac{\log_b(x)}{\log_b(a)}\) (change of base) — left as an exercise.

§5.3Limits

Limit of a function

\(\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=c\) if, for any \(\epsilon>0\), there exists \(\delta>0\) such that \(|f(x)-c|<\epsilon\) whenever \(|x-x_0|<\delta\).

Most of the time you won't have to deal with this definition: for the functions we use (you'll learn at university that these are continuous functions), \(\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=f(x_0)\) — what is \(\lim_{x\to1}x^2\)? It's \(1\). We write \(f(x)\to c\) for easily understandable limits. The interesting cases involve \(\infty\):

Behaviour of quotients
  1. If \(f(x)\to\infty\) and \(g(x)\) is bounded or has a finite limit, then \(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\to\infty\).
  2. If \(f(x)\) has a finite non-zero limit (or is bounded below by a positive quantity) and \(g(x)\to0\), then \(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\to\pm\infty\), depending on signs.
  3. If \(f(x)\) is bounded or has a finite limit and \(g(x)\to\infty\), then \(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\to0\).

What about \(\frac\infty\infty\)? Simplify until only one function goes to infinity. For \(\frac{x^2+2}{x^3+6}\) as \(x\to\infty\): factor out \(x^2\) to get \(\frac{1+2x^{-2}}{x+6x^{-2}}\) — numerator \(\to1\), denominator \(\to\infty\), so the fraction \(\to0\). A hierarchy of growth to remember:

Growth hierarchy
  1. The exponential grows fastest: \(\frac{e^x}{f(x)}\to\infty\) for polynomial or logarithmic \(f\).
  2. If \(f(x)\to\infty\) then \(\frac1{f(x)}\to0\).
  3. If \(\deg f=\deg g+k\) with \(k\ge1\), then \(\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\to\infty\).
  4. Logarithms grow slowest: \(\frac{\log x}{f(x)}\to0\) for polynomial or exponential \(f\).

Further properties (without proof): limits respect sums and differences; \(\lim fg=\lim f\cdot\lim g\) when the limits are finite; and \(\lim\frac fg=\frac{\lim f}{\lim g}\) whenever the limits aren't \(0\) or \(\infty\) (if the denominator's is \(0\) and the numerator's is \(\pm\infty\), the quotient's is \(\pm\infty\)).

§5.4Asymptotes

Look closely at \(f(x)=\frac1x\) on the positive axis: the function goes to 0 as \(x\to\infty\), and this convergence can be "approximated" by a line. The general idea: an asymptote is a line that approximates a function as it approaches its limit.

The three types of asymptote
  1. Horizontal: if \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=a\), the line \(y=a\) is a horizontal asymptote at \(+\infty\) (similarly at \(-\infty\)).
  2. Vertical: if \(\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)=\pm\infty\), the line \(x=x_0\) is a vertical asymptote.
  3. Oblique: \(f(x)\) approaches the line \(y=mx+b\) as \(x\to\infty\), formalised as \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\big(f(x)-mx-b\big)=0\).

Calculating asymptotes is especially easy for rational functions \(f(x)=\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\) with \(\deg p=n\), \(\deg q=m\):

Template — Asymptotes of a rational function
  1. If \(n\ge m+2\): no horizontal or oblique asymptotes; vertical asymptotes at all roots of \(q\) that aren't roots of \(p\).
  2. If \(n=m+1\): no horizontal asymptote; vertical asymptotes as above; an oblique asymptote whose slope is the ratio of leading coefficients \(\frac{a_{m+1}}{b_m}\).
  3. If \(n=m\): a horizontal asymptote \(y=\frac{a_m}{b_m}\) (ratio of leading coefficients), plus vertical asymptotes as above.

§5.6Derivatives and turning points

The end goal of this chapter is to be able to draw the graph of any function — and for that we need to see how the function increases and decreases. Comparing \(f(x)-f(y)\) for \(x>y\) tells us it increases somewhere between the points; to capture the direction at a point, we'd want \(x\to y\), but then \(f(x)\to f(y)\) and the difference vanishes. The concept of the derivative was created to model these small changes at specific points — the definition must involve both the change in the function's values and the change on the \(x\)-axis:

Definition — Derivative

For \(f:X\to Y\) and a point \(x\), if the limit \[f'(x)=\lim_{y\to x}\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{y-x}\] exists, it is called the derivative of \(f\) at \(x\). An equally useful equivalent form is \(f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h\). (Convince yourself the two are equivalent.)

Not every function is differentiable. Assume a continuous function is one you can graph without lifting your pen — Karl Weierstrass (1815–1897), the father of modern analysis, discovered a continuous function differentiable at no point:

We needn't look that far: if \(\lim_{x\to x_0}f(x)\ne f(x_0)\), the function isn't differentiable at \(x_0\) — the numerator of \(\frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0}\) stays away from 0 while the denominator goes to 0.

Sign of the derivative

If \(f\) is differentiable and \(f'(x_0)>0\), the function is increasing at \(x_0\); if \(f'(x_0)<0\), it is decreasing.

When \(f'(x_0)=0\), the derivative may change sign at \(x_0\) — giving a local maximum (positive→negative) or local minimum (negative→positive) — or keep its sign, giving a saddle/stationary inflection point. Since we can't check infinitely many nearby points, we use the second derivative:

Template — Classifying a point x₀
  1. Compute \(f'(x_0)\): positive → increasing; negative → decreasing.
  2. If \(f'(x_0)=0\): compute \(f''(x_0)\). If \(f''(x_0)>0\) — minimum; if \(f''(x_0)<0\) — maximum; if \(f''(x_0)=0\) — differentiate further and check the signs of higher derivatives.

§5.7Graphs of functions

One property can dramatically simplify a sketch: periodicity. A function is periodic if \(f(x)=f(x+t)\) for all \(x\) (period \(t\)); \(\sin x\) has period \(2\pi\). If a function is periodic, draw it on one period and copy.

Periodic functions cannot converge at infinity, so they have no horizontal asymptotes. Periodicity can act in more arguments too: for the curve \(\sin x=\cos y\), if \((x,y)\) is on the graph then so are \((x+2\pi,y)\) and \((x,y+2\pi)\) — the picture is periodic in both directions. Such graphs typically appear in the TMUA's multiple-choice questions.

Template — Drawing the graph of a function
  1. Decide the set on which the function is defined (e.g. \(\frac1x\) is not defined at 0 — don't draw it there).
  2. Search for periodicity; if periodic, work inside one period.
  3. Search for asymptotes and limits at infinity. Vertical asymptotes lurk at points where the function isn't defined — check those with care.
  4. Find where the function increases/decreases, the maxima, minima and turning points, using the derivative.
  5. Plot a few points to fix the shape of the curve.

Example. Graph \(\sin^2x\). The output lies in \([0,1]\). Since \(\sin(x+\pi)=-\sin x\), squaring gives period \(\pi\) (not \(2\pi\)) — so work on \([0,\pi]\). Defined everywhere and bounded: no asymptotes of any kind (periodicity also rules out horizontal ones). Differentiate: \((\sin^2x)'=2\sin x\cos x=\sin2x\), positive on \(\left(0,\frac\pi2\right)\), zero at \(\frac\pi2\), negative after — a maximum at \(x=\frac\pi2\). Plot a few points and copy the bump along the axis.

§5.9Tangents and normals to graphs

Take two points \(A(x_a,f(x_a))\) and \(B(x_b,f(x_b))\) on a graph and form the right triangle with the horizontal. The tangent of the angle at the base is \(\frac{f(x_b)-f(x_a)}{x_b-x_a}\) — very close to the definition of the derivative. As \(B\) slides toward \(A\), the limit of this ratio is \(f'(x_a)\): the derivative is the slope of the tangent line to the graph at \(A\).

Equation of the tangent at \(A\). A line is \(y=mx+b\); the slope is \(m=f'(x_a)\), and since the line passes through \(A\): \(f(x_a)=f'(x_a)x_a+b\), so \(b=f(x_a)-f'(x_a)x_a\). Equivalently, \[y=f(x_a)+f'(x_a)(x-x_a).\]

Normal of a graph

The normal of a graph at point \(A\) is the line perpendicular to the tangent at \(A\). If two lines with slopes \(m\) and \(m'\) are perpendicular, then \(m\cdot m'=-1\); so the slope of the normal is \(\dfrac{-1}{f'(x_a)}\), and we proceed as before using the fact that the normal passes through \(A\).

§5.11Geometric transformations

§5.11.1 Translations

From the graph of \(f(x)\), what other graphs can we obtain? The simplest idea: move it around. Adding to the output translates vertically: the graph of \(f(x)+a\) is the graph of \(f\) moved up by \(a\) units. Horizontal translations hide a trap: to move the graph to the right by 1, the new graph is not \(f(x+1)\) but \(f(x-1)\) — adding inside the bracket moves the graph left, since \(f((x-1)+1)=f(x)\). Generally, \(f(x-a)\) is the graph shifted right by \(a\) (with negative \(a\) giving a shift left by \(|a|\)).

§5.11.2 Dilation and contraction

What is \(f(ax)\)? Let \(g(x)=f(ax)\); then \(g\!\left(\frac xa\right)=f(x)\), so the point \(x\) becomes \(\frac xa\): we are contracting the graph horizontally by a factor \(a\). Multiplying outside the function, \(a\,f(x)\), dilates the graph vertically — for instance \(2\sin x\) doubles the amplitude of the wave, while \(\sin 2x\) squashes it to half the wavelength. Vertical changes (adding or multiplying outside) are the easy ones, as nothing changes on the \(x\)-axis; horizontal changes (inside the bracket) need more care.

New functionEffect on the graph of \(f(x)\)
\(f(x)+a\)translate up by \(a\)
\(f(x-a)\)translate right by \(a\)
\(a\,f(x)\)vertical stretch by factor \(a\)
\(f(ax)\)horizontal squash by factor \(a\)
\(-f(x)\)reflect in the \(x\)-axis
\(f(-x)\)reflect in the \(y\)-axis

§5.12Functional equations

We conclude with functional equations — a topic with little prerequisite theory beyond knowing how to calculate. A functional equation has the shape \(g(f(\mathbf x),\mathbf x)=C\), where \(\mathbf x\) may be a set of variables: \(f(x+y)-x=y\) is a proper functional equation in both \(x\) and \(y\). Two problem types arise.

Type 1: find a specific value. Asked for \(f(3)\)? Replace \(x\) with 3 and see what happens; you'll usually get an equation linking \(f(3)\) to other values of \(f\), and chasing those values closes the loop.

Example. \(f\) is defined on the naturals with \(f(1)=1\), \(f(2n)=f(n)\) and \(f(2n+1)=f(n)+1\). What is \(f(9)\)? We have \(f(9)=f(4)+1\), \(f(4)=f(2)\), \(f(2)=f(1)=1\). So \(f(9)=2\).

Type 2: find \(f\) everywhere. Give values to the unknowns — trying \(0\) and \(1\) usually leads in the correct direction.

Example (Cauchy equation). Let \(f\) satisfy \(f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)\) on the reals. Find \(f\) on the rationals.

Solution. \(x=y=1\) gives \(f(2)=2f(1)\); then \(f(3)=3f(1)\), and by induction \(f(n)=nf(1)\). With \(x=y=0\): \(f(0)=2f(0)\), so \(f(0)=0\); with \(x=1,y=-1\): \(f(-1)=-f(1)\), and similarly \(f(-n)=-nf(1)\), so \(f(z)=zf(1)\) on the integers. Prove by induction that \(f(x_1+\dots+x_n)=f(x_1)+\dots+f(x_n)\) (an easy exercise); then \(f(m)=nf\!\left(\frac mn\right)\), so \(f\!\left(\frac mn\right)=\frac mn f(1)\). Thus \(f(q)=qf(1)\) for every rational \(q\). At university you will learn that under mild extra conditions (continuity, monotonicity, …) the same holds for all reals. ∎
