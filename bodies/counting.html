Counting, Probability and Statistics

Arrangements, selections, and the binomial machinery for extracting coefficients — plus the probability and statistics the TMUA assumes as "underpinning knowledge". It appears inside Paper 1 questions rather than as a topic of its own, which makes it the easiest place to lose unexpected marks.

§C.1Counting

The multiplication principle

If a task splits into stages with \(m\) choices at the first stage and \(n\) at the second (whatever was chosen first), the number of outcomes is \(m\times n\). Everything below is this principle applied repeatedly.

Arrangements and selections
  1. Arrangements of \(n\) distinct objects: \(n!=1\cdot2\cdots n\) (with \(0!=1\)).
  2. Arrangements of \(r\) objects from \(n\): \(\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}\) — order matters.
  3. Selections of \(r\) objects from \(n\): \(\dbinom nr=\dfrac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}\) — order doesn't matter; divide the arrangements by the \(r!\) orderings of each selection. This is the same \(\binom nr\) as in the Binomial Theorem — the coefficient of \(x^r\) in \((1+x)^n\) counts the ways of choosing which \(r\) brackets contribute an \(x\).
  4. Arrangements with repeats: \(n\) objects of which groups of \(k_1,k_2,\dots\) are identical: \(\dfrac{n!}{k_1!\,k_2!\cdots}\).

Example. How many "words" can be formed from the letters of OXBRIDGE? Eight distinct letters: \(8!=40320\). And from BANANA? \(\frac{6!}{3!\,2!}=60\) — six letters with three A's and two N's identical.

The slickest counting tool in multiple-choice settings is complementary counting: when "at least one…" appears, count the opposite. How many of the \(2^{10}\) binary strings of length 10 contain at least one 1? All but one: \(2^{10}-1\).

§C.2Extracting coefficients: the Binomial Theorem at work

The single most common counting question on TMUA Paper 1 doesn't look like a counting question at all: "find the coefficient of \(x^a\) in the expansion of …". It is counting — a coefficient counts the number of (weighted) ways of picking terms from brackets — and the Binomial Theorem is the machine that does the count.

The general term

From \((a+b)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk a^{n-k}b^k\), the expansion of \(\left(a+bx^m\right)^n\) has general term \[\binom nk\,a^{n-k}\,b^k\,x^{mk}.\] To find the coefficient of a target power: solve for \(k\) first (set \(mk=\) target), then evaluate that single term — never expand the whole bracket. The same discipline finds the term independent of \(x\) in expressions like \(\left(x+\frac2{x^2}\right)^9\): the general term carries \(x^{9-k}\cdot x^{-2k}=x^{9-3k}\), so the constant term is the one with \(k=3\).

Template — Coefficients in \((a+f(x))^n\) with \(f\) a polynomial
  1. Treat \(f(x)\) as a single object: the expansion is \(\sum_k\binom nk a^{n-k}f(x)^k\) — a binomial in disguise, no multinomial theorem needed.
  2. Work out which values of \(k\) can contribute to the target power before expanding anything: if the lowest power in \(f\) is \(x^p\) and the highest is \(x^q\), then \(f(x)^k\) produces powers between \(x^{pk}\) and \(x^{qk}\), so only \(k\) with \(pk\le\text{target}\le qk\) matter — usually two or three values.
  3. For each surviving \(k\), extract the needed coefficient of \(f(x)^k\) (itself a small binomial if \(f\) has two terms), multiply by \(\binom nk a^{n-k}\), and add up.
  4. If the expression is a product of brackets, e.g. \((1+2x)^3(2-x)^4\), expand each general term separately and sum over the ways the powers can combine: coefficient of \(x^a\) \(=\sum_{i+j=a}\)(coeff of \(x^i\) in the first)(coeff of \(x^j\) in the second).
  5. Always carry the signs inside the bracket with the term — a stray minus is the genre's signature lost mark.

Worked example. Find the coefficient of \(x^3\) in \(\left(2+x-x^2\right)^5\).

Solution (nested binomial). Write the bracket as \(\big(2+x(1-x)\big)^5\), with general term \(\binom5k2^{5-k}x^k(1-x)^k\), producing powers from \(x^k\) to \(x^{2k}\). For \(x^3\) we need \(k\le3\le2k\): only \(k=2\) and \(k=3\) contribute.
\(k=2\): \(\binom52\,2^3\,x^2(1-x)^2\) — we need the coefficient of \(x^1\) in \((1-x)^2\), which is \(-2\): contribution \(10\cdot8\cdot(-2)=-160\).
\(k=3\): \(\binom53\,2^2\,x^3(1-x)^3\) — we need the constant term of \((1-x)^3\), which is \(1\): contribution \(10\cdot4\cdot1=40\).
Total: \(-160+40=\mathbf{-120}\). ∎
Solution (factorising first). Spot that \(2+x-x^2=(2-x)(1+x)\), so the expression is \((2-x)^5(1+x)^5\), and apply step 4 of the template: \[\sum_{i+j=3}\left[\binom5i2^{5-i}(-1)^i\right]\binom5j =32\cdot10\;-\;80\cdot10\;+\;80\cdot5\;-\;40\cdot1=-120.\] Same answer, and a reminder that thirty seconds spent looking for a factorisation can simplify the bookkeeping. ∎

§C.3Probability

Probability

For equally likely outcomes, \(\mathbb P(A)=\dfrac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}\) — which is why probability questions are usually counting questions wearing a disguise. Always \(0\le\mathbb P(A)\le1\), and \(\mathbb P(\text{not }A)=1-\mathbb P(A)\).

Combining events
  1. \(\mathbb P(A\text{ or }B)=\mathbb P(A)+\mathbb P(B)-\mathbb P(A\text{ and }B)\); for mutually exclusive events the last term is 0.
  2. For independent events, \(\mathbb P(A\text{ and }B)=\mathbb P(A)\,\mathbb P(B)\). Successive draws without replacement are not independent — update the counts; a tree diagram keeps the bookkeeping honest.

Example. Two cards are drawn without replacement from a standard pack. \(\mathbb P(\text{both aces})=\frac4{52}\cdot\frac3{51}=\frac1{221}\). With replacement it would be \(\left(\frac1{13}\right)^2=\frac1{169}\) — the difference is precisely the loss of independence.

Example (complementary counting again). A fair die is rolled 4 times. \(\mathbb P(\text{at least one six})=1-\left(\frac56\right)^4=\frac{671}{1296}\approx0.52\) — de Méré's classic, and the standard pattern for every "at least one" probability.

§C.4Statistics

Measures to know
  1. Mean: \(\bar x=\frac1n\sum x_i\). Adding a constant to every datum adds it to the mean; scaling every datum scales the mean.
  2. Median: the middle value when sorted (average of the middle two for even \(n\)) — robust to outliers, unlike the mean.
  3. Mode: the most frequent value.
  4. Range (max − min) and interquartile range (upper minus lower quartile) measure spread.

TMUA questions on this material are reasoning questions: "the mean of five positive integers is 10 and their median is 12 — what is the largest possible value of the greatest of them?" (Fix the sum at 50, push the two below the median down to 1 each and the one between median and maximum down to 12: \(50-1-1-12-12=24\).) Reading information off charts — bar charts, pie charts, cumulative frequency — is also assumed; the mathematics is never deeper than proportional reasoning, but read the axes.

§C.5Problems

Question 1New

How many even four-digit numbers can be formed using the digits 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 with no digit repeated?

Show solution

Constrain the most restricted position first: the last digit must be 2 or 4 (2 ways); the remaining three positions take an arrangement of 3 of the remaining 4 digits: \(4\cdot3\cdot2=24\). Total \(2\cdot24=48\).

Question 2New · TMUA-style

A committee of 4 is chosen from 6 men and 5 women. In how many ways can it be chosen so that it contains at least one woman?

Show solution

Complementary counting: all committees minus all-male committees: \(\binom{11}4-\binom64=330-15=315\).

Question 3New

In the expansion of \((2+x)^6\), find the coefficient of \(x^4\), and verify that the sum of all seven coefficients equals \(3^6\) without computing them individually.

Show solution

The \(x^4\) term is \(\binom64\,2^2x^4=15\cdot4\,x^4\): coefficient 60. Setting \(x=1\) in the expansion sums the coefficients: \((2+1)^6=3^6=729\) — the substitution trick from MAT 2019 (Sequences, Question 8).

Question 4New · TMUA-style

A bag contains 5 red and 3 blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. Given that \(\mathbb P(\text{both red})=\frac{5}{14}\), find \(\mathbb P(\text{the two balls have different colours})\), and decide: are the events "first ball red" and "second ball red" independent?

Show solution

\(\mathbb P(\text{both blue})=\frac38\cdot\frac27=\frac3{28}\). Different colours: \(1-\frac5{14}-\frac3{28}=1-\frac{10}{28}-\frac3{28}=\frac{15}{28}\). Independence fails: \(\mathbb P(\text{2nd red}\mid\text{1st red})=\frac47\ne\frac58=\mathbb P(\text{1st red})\) — drawing without replacement changes the composition of the bag.

Question 5New · Paper 2 flavour

A student argues: "When I roll two dice, the total is one of the eleven values \(2,3,\dots,12\); therefore each total has probability \(\frac1{11}\)." Identify the error and compute the correct probability that the total is 7.

Show solution

The error: the eleven totals are not equally likely — the equally-likely outcomes are the \(36\) ordered pairs. The total 7 arises from \((1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)\): \(\mathbb P=\frac6{36}=\frac16\). Equal likelihood is a property to be justified, never assumed from a mere list of outcomes.

Question 6New · TMUA-style

Five positive integers have mean 7, median 8, a unique mode of 9, and range 6. Find the five integers, and explain why the answer is unique.

Show solution

The sum is 35 and, sorted, the list is \(a,b,8,d,e\). A unique mode of 9 forces at least two 9s, which can only sit above the median: \(d=e=9\). The maximum is 9, so range 6 gives \(a=3\); then \(b=35-3-8-9-9=6\), and \(3\le6\le8\) ✓. The integers are \(\{3,6,8,9,9\}\). Each condition pinned down one unknown in turn — mode placed the 9s, range fixed \(a\), the mean fixed \(b\) — so no other list is possible. (Exercise in the spirit of Paper 2: show that changing "range 6" to "range 10" makes the question unsatisfiable, since the maximum is forced to be 9 and \(a\ge1\) caps the range at 8.)

Question 7New · TMUA-style

Find the term independent of \(x\) in the expansion of \(\left(x+\dfrac2{x^2}\right)^9\).

Show solution

General term: \(\binom9k x^{9-k}\left(\frac2{x^2}\right)^k=\binom9k2^kx^{9-3k}\). Independent of \(x\) needs \(9-3k=0\), i.e. \(k=3\): the term is \(\binom93\cdot2^3=84\cdot8=672\).

Question 8New · TMUA-style

Find the coefficient of \(x^4\) in the expansion of \(\left(1+x+x^2\right)^5\).

Show solution

Write \(\big(1+x(1+x)\big)^5\) with general term \(\binom5k x^k(1+x)^k\), spanning powers \(x^k\) to \(x^{2k}\); for \(x^4\), \(k=2,3,4\) contribute.
\(k=2\): need coeff of \(x^2\) in \((1+x)^2\): \(1\) → \(\binom52\cdot1=10\).
\(k=3\): need coeff of \(x^1\) in \((1+x)^3\): \(3\) → \(\binom53\cdot3=30\).
\(k=4\): need the constant of \((1+x)^4\): \(1\) → \(\binom54\cdot1=5\).
Total: \(10+30+5=45\).

Question 9New · TMUA-style

In the expansion of \(\left(1+ax+2x^2\right)^4\), the coefficient of \(x^2\) is 32. Given \(a>0\), find \(a\).

Show solution

Treat \(f(x)=ax+2x^2\): the expansion is \(\sum_k\binom4kf(x)^k\). The \(x^2\) contributions: \(k=1\) supplies the \(2x^2\) inside \(f\), giving \(\binom41\cdot2=8\); \(k=2\) supplies \((ax)^2\) from \(f(x)^2\), giving \(\binom42a^2=6a^2\). So \(6a^2+8=32\), \(a^2=4\), and with \(a>0\): \(a=2\).
