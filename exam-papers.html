TMUA Exam Papers

Working past papers under timed conditions is the single highest-value activity in your preparation — theory tells you what to do, papers teach you to do it in 75 seconds.

Official past papers

The TMUA exam papers from 2017 to 2023 are available online, as well as a specimen paper and a practice paper (the latter is sometimes labelled as the 2016 paper). These can be found, with answers and sometimes worked solutions, at:

TMUA–ESAT official preparation materials → Physics and Maths Tutor — TMUA past papers →

Since 2024 the test is delivered online at Pearson VUE test centres, so there is no conventional 2024 paper. Pearson provides sample tests online so candidates can get familiar with the on-screen format and navigation — choose TMUA from the list of exams on their University Admissions Tests page:

Pearson VUE — TMUA specimen and timed practice tests →

Other excellent practice sources

Although the MAT was discontinued after its 2025 sitting (Oxford now uses the TMUA), its archive of past papers remains first-rate practice for the TMUA's multiple-choice format. STEP material trains the same reasoning at greater depth. Many of the problems on this site are drawn from these sources:

Oxford — MAT past-paper archive and solutions (discontinued test, excellent practice) → STEP past papers and preparation resources → The STEP Support Programme (University of Cambridge) → Tyler Tutoring — four sets of TMUA mock papers with solutions →

How to use past papers

Template — A past-paper session
  1. Simulate the exam: 75 minutes per paper, no calculator (none is allowed in the TMUA), no notes, answers on paper before checking.
  2. Mark honestly, then sort every dropped mark into one of three bins: knowledge (didn't know the fact — go to the relevant topic page), technique (knew it, executed slowly or wrongly — drill that template), or logic (misread the question, fell for a converse, missed a case — revisit the Logic and Proof topic).
  3. Redo every wrong question from scratch a few days later. A question you got wrong and then understood is worth five you got right first time.
  4. Keep a running list of your personal traps — everyone's list is different, and by the third paper, yours will have stopped surprising you.

As discussed on the home page: prioritise accuracy on multiple choice, leave your slowest question type for last, and remember that the student who gets accepted is the one who has developed enough intuition and experience to solve problems quickly under exam conditions. Mathematics isn't a spectator sport.
