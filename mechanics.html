Mechanics

Two of the twelve questions on every STEP paper are mechanics — and they are the least popular questions on the paper, which is precisely why you should learn to love them.

§M.1Introduction

As discussed on the home page: STEP lets you choose 6 questions from 12, of which 2 are mechanics and 2 are statistics, and these four are the most unpopular questions on the paper. Knowing them well gives you more leverage than just knowing more pure mathematics — you gain four candidate questions your competitors have written off. Better still, STEP mechanics is not really a new subject: it is the calculus and trigonometry you already have, applied to particles. The physics input is small; the problem-solving is the same.

§M.2Kinematics

A particle moves along a line with displacement \(x(t)\). Velocity and acceleration are nothing but derivatives: \[v=\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}=\dot x,\qquad a=\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=\ddot x,\] (the dot notation, from Newton himself, already appeared in the STEP problems of the Differentiation topic). Going backwards: displacement is the integral of velocity — the area under the \(v\)–\(t\) graph — and velocity is the integral of acceleration.

When the acceleration is a constant \(a\), integrating twice produces the famous suvat equations. Don't memorise them as five arbitrary facts — derive them once and own them:

The suvat equations (constant acceleration only)

Integrating \(\dot v=a\) with \(v(0)=u\): \(v=u+at\). Integrating again with \(x(0)=0\): \[s=ut+\tfrac12at^2.\] Eliminating \(t\) between these gives \(v^2=u^2+2as\); averaging gives \(s=\frac{(u+v)t}2\). Here \(s\) = displacement, \(u\) = initial velocity, \(v\) = final velocity.

The trap

suvat is valid only for constant acceleration. The moment a force depends on position, velocity or time — springs, resistance proportional to \(v\), variable thrust — you must return to the differential equations of the Differentiation topic. A standard STEP move for position-dependent acceleration is the chain-rule identity \[a=\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dx}\cdot\frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt}=v\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\!\left(\tfrac12v^2\right),\] which converts time-equations into separable space-equations.

§M.3Projectiles

Launch a particle with speed \(u\) at angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal, with gravity \(g\) acting downwards and no air resistance. The single governing idea: the two components don't talk to each other. Horizontally there is no force, so the velocity component \(u\cos\theta\) is constant; vertically the particle does one-dimensional suvat with acceleration \(-g\): \[x=ut\cos\theta,\qquad y=ut\sin\theta-\tfrac12gt^2.\]

The standard results (derive, don't memorise)
  1. Time of flight (returning to launch height): set \(y=0\): \(T=\dfrac{2u\sin\theta}{g}\).
  2. Range: \(R=uT\cos\theta=\dfrac{u^2\sin2\theta}{g}\) — maximised at \(\theta=45^\circ\) by the double-angle formula from the Geometry topic.
  3. Greatest height: vertical velocity zero gives \(H=\dfrac{u^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}\).
  4. Trajectory: eliminate \(t\): \[y=x\tan\theta-\frac{gx^2}{2u^2\cos^2\theta}=x\tan\theta-\frac{gx^2}{2u^2}\left(1+\tan^2\theta\right).\] The second form, using \(\sec^2\theta=1+\tan^2\theta\), is the STEP workhorse: for fixed target \((x,y)\) it is a quadratic in \(\tan\theta\), so the discriminant decides which points are reachable — geometry of the parabola meeting the algebra of Topic 2.

§M.4Forces and Newton's laws

Newton's laws, exam edition
  1. No resultant force \(\Rightarrow\) no acceleration (equilibrium: forces balance in every direction).
  2. \(\mathbf F=m\mathbf a\) — the resultant force equals mass times acceleration, applied componentwise.
  3. If \(A\) pushes \(B\), then \(B\) pushes \(A\) equally and oppositely — the law that makes connected-particle problems work.

The standard cast of forces: weight \(mg\) downwards; the normal reaction \(R\) perpendicular to a surface; tension along a string (the same throughout a light string over a smooth pulley); and friction, which opposes relative motion and obeys \[F\le\mu R,\] with equality (limiting friction) when the object is on the point of slipping or already sliding. The inequality is the point: a static object's friction takes whatever value equilibrium demands, up to the ceiling \(\mu R\).

Template — Forces on an inclined plane
  1. Draw the diagram and mark every force on the particle: weight, normal, friction, applied forces. (Unmarked forces are the leading cause of lost STEP marks.)
  2. Choose axes along and perpendicular to the slope. The weight resolves into \(mg\sin\alpha\) down the slope and \(mg\cos\alpha\) into it.
  3. Perpendicular to the slope, nothing accelerates: \(R=mg\cos\alpha\) (adjusted for any other forces with perpendicular components).
  4. Along the slope, apply \(F=ma\) (or equilibrium), with friction's direction set by the motion — or, in "on the point of moving" problems, by the threatened motion.
  5. Sliding begins when the slope is steep enough that \(mg\sin\alpha>\mu mg\cos\alpha\), i.e. \(\tan\alpha>\mu\) — the angle of friction.
Template — Connected particles and pulleys
  1. Write \(F=ma\) for each particle separately, in its own direction of motion, with the same \(a\) (an inextensible string) and the same \(T\) (a light string, smooth pulley).
  2. Add the equations to eliminate \(T\) and find \(a\); substitute back for \(T\).
  3. Sanity-check limits: if one mass dominates, \(a\to g\); if the masses are equal (on a simple pulley), \(a=0\).

Example. Masses \(m_1>m_2\) hang from a light string over a smooth pulley. For \(m_1\): \(m_1g-T=m_1a\); for \(m_2\): \(T-m_2g=m_2a\). Adding: \[a=\frac{(m_1-m_2)g}{m_1+m_2},\qquad T=\frac{2m_1m_2g}{m_1+m_2}.\] Check the limits as in step 3 — they pass.

§M.5Work, energy and power

The energy dictionary

Work done by a constant force: \(W=Fd\cos\theta\) (force times distance, counted along the force); for a variable force along a line, \(W=\int F\,\mathrm dx\). Kinetic energy: \(\frac12mv^2\). Gravitational potential energy: \(mgh\). Power is the rate of working: \(P=\frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt}=Fv\) — the formula behind every "car driving up a hill at constant speed" question.

The work–energy principle

The total work done on a particle equals its change in kinetic energy: \[\int F\,\mathrm dx=\tfrac12mv^2-\tfrac12mu^2\] — which is exactly the identity \(F=ma=m\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\left(\frac12v^2\right)\) of §M.2, integrated. When the only forces are gravity and reactions perpendicular to motion, this becomes conservation of energy: \(\frac12mv^2+mgh\) is constant. Friction and resistance drain the account: energy lost \(=\) work done against them.

Energy is the great shortcut: it relates speeds to positions without solving for the motion in time. If a STEP question asks "how fast at the bottom?" and not "when?", reach for energy before kinematics.

§M.6Momentum, impulse and collisions

Momentum and impulse

Momentum: \(mv\) (a vector). Impulse of a force: \(\int F\,\mathrm dt\), equal to the change in momentum — Newton's second law integrated in time, the partner of the work–energy principle (which integrates it in space).

Collisions
  1. Conservation of momentum: with no external impulse, total momentum is unchanged: \(m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2\). (Take a positive direction and stick to it — sign errors are the genre's signature mistake.)
  2. Newton's law of restitution: the speed of separation is \(e\) times the speed of approach, \[v_2-v_1=e\,(u_1-u_2),\qquad 0\le e\le1,\] with \(e=1\) a perfectly elastic collision (kinetic energy conserved) and \(e=0\) perfectly inelastic (the particles coalesce). For a ball bouncing off a fixed wall or floor: rebound speed \(=e\times\) impact speed — whence the classic bouncing-ball geometric series, which is why this section follows the Sequences topic's geometric sums.
  3. Kinetic energy is not conserved unless \(e=1\); computing the energy lost is a standard final part.

§M.7Problems

Question 1New

A particle moves with \(v(t)=6t-3t^2\) (metres per second) for \(t\ge0\). Find (i) its displacement when it first returns to its starting point, justifying your answer; (ii) the total distance travelled by \(t=3\).

Show solution

Displacement: \(x(t)=3t^2-t^3\), zero at \(t=0\) and \(t=3\) — so (i) the displacement at first return is, by definition, 0, at \(t=3\). (ii) \(v=0\) at \(t=2\); the particle goes forward to \(x(2)=4\), then back to \(x(3)=0\): total distance \(4+4=8\) m. Distance is the integral of \(|v|\) — split at the turning point, exactly like the signed-area discussion in the Integration topic.

Question 2New

A ball is projected from ground level with speed \(u\) at angle \(\theta\). Show that the maximum height and the range satisfy \(H=\frac R4\tan\theta\), and deduce the angle for which the ball's greatest height equals its range.

Show solution

\(\frac HR=\frac{u^2\sin^2\theta/2g}{u^2\cdot2\sin\theta\cos\theta/g}=\frac{\sin\theta}{4\cos\theta}=\frac{\tan\theta}4\), giving the identity. \(H=R\) requires \(\tan\theta=4\), i.e. \(\theta=\arctan4\approx76^\circ\).

Question 3New · STEP-style

A particle is projected with speed \(u\) from the origin and must pass through the point \((a,b)\). Using the trajectory equation in its \(\tan\theta\) form, show that this is possible if and only if \[b\le\frac{u^2}{2g}-\frac{ga^2}{2u^2},\] and interpret the boundary curve geometrically.

Show solution

Substituting \((a,b)\) into \(y=x\tan\theta-\frac{gx^2}{2u^2}(1+\tan^2\theta)\) and writing \(T=\tan\theta\): \[\frac{ga^2}{2u^2}T^2-aT+\left(b+\frac{ga^2}{2u^2}\right)=0,\] a quadratic in \(T\) with real solutions iff its discriminant is non-negative: \(a^2\ge4\cdot\frac{ga^2}{2u^2}\left(b+\frac{ga^2}{2u^2}\right)\), which rearranges to the stated inequality. The boundary \(y=\frac{u^2}{2g}-\frac{gx^2}{2u^2}\) is the parabola of safety: the envelope of all trajectories — points below it can be hit (generally by two angles, the flat and the lobbed shot), points above it by none.

Question 4New

A block of mass \(m\) rests on a rough plane inclined at angle \(\alpha\), with coefficient of friction \(\mu>\tan\alpha\). A force \(P\) is applied up the slope, along it. Find the range of values of \(P\) for which the block remains in equilibrium.

Show solution

Perpendicular: \(R=mg\cos\alpha\). Along the slope, friction can act either way with \(|F|\le\mu mg\cos\alpha\). Equilibrium requires \(P-mg\sin\alpha=-F\), i.e. \(|P-mg\sin\alpha|\le\mu mg\cos\alpha\): \[mg(\sin\alpha-\mu\cos\alpha)\le P\le mg(\sin\alpha+\mu\cos\alpha).\] Since \(\mu>\tan\alpha\), the lower bound is negative, so every \(0\le P\le mg(\sin\alpha+\mu\cos\alpha)\) works — including \(P=0\), consistent with the block sitting still unaided. Push harder than the upper bound and it slides up.

Question 5New

A car of mass 1200 kg has a maximum engine power of 60 kW and experiences resistance of magnitude \(40v\) newtons at speed \(v\). Find its maximum speed on the flat, and its maximum steady speed up a slope of inclination \(\arcsin\frac1{20}\).

Show solution

On the flat at top speed the driving force equals resistance: \(\frac{60000}v=40v\), so \(v^2=1500\), \(v=10\sqrt{15}\approx38.7\) m s(^{-1}). Uphill, the engine also fights the weight component \(1200g\cdot\frac1{20}=60g\approx588\) N: \[\frac{60000}v=40v+588\ \Rightarrow\ 40v^2+588v-60000=0,\] giving \(v\approx31.9\) m s(^{-1}) (the positive root). The structure \(P=Fv\) plus a force balance is the whole genre.

Question 6New · STEP-style

A particle of mass \(m\) moving with speed \(u\) strikes directly a stationary particle of mass \(km\) (\(k>0\)), with coefficient of restitution \(e\).
(i) Find the velocities after impact. (ii) Show that the fraction of kinetic energy lost is \(\dfrac{k(1-e^2)}{1+k}\). (iii) For which \(e\) and \(k\) does the first particle rebound (reverse direction)?

Show solution

(i) Momentum: \(mu=mv_1+kmv_2\); restitution: \(v_2-v_1=eu\). Solving: \(v_1=\dfrac{(1-ke)u}{1+k}\), \(v_2=\dfrac{(1+e)u}{1+k}\).
(ii) Energy after: \(\frac12m v_1^2+\frac12kmv_2^2\); a (worthwhile) algebra exercise gives loss \(\frac12mu^2\cdot\frac{k(1-e^2)}{1+k}\) — zero iff \(e=1\), as it must be.
(iii) \(v_1<0\iff ke>1\): the struck particle must be heavy enough and the collision bouncy enough.

Question 7New

A ball is dropped from height \(h\) onto a fixed floor, with coefficient of restitution \(e\) at each bounce. Using the geometric sums of the Sequences topic, show that the total distance travelled before the ball comes to rest is \[h\,\frac{1+e^2}{1-e^2},\] and find the total time taken.

Show solution

Impact speed from height \(h\) is \(\sqrt{2gh}\); rebound speed \(e\sqrt{2gh}\) gives rebound height \(e^2h\), then \(e^4h\), and so on. Distance: \(h+2e^2h+2e^4h+\dots=h+\frac{2e^2h}{1-e^2}=h\frac{1+e^2}{1-e^2}\). Times: the first drop takes \(\sqrt{2h/g}\), and each subsequent up-and-down from height \(e^{2n}h\) takes \(2\sqrt{2e^{2n}h/g}\) — a geometric series in \(e^n\): \[T=\sqrt{\frac{2h}g}\left(1+\frac{2e}{1-e}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{2h}g}\cdot\frac{1+e}{1-e}.\] Infinitely many bounces, finite distance, finite time — Zeno would approve.

Question 8STEP-style · Hard

A particle moves in a straight line against a resistance proportional to its speed, so \(\dot v=-kv\) with \(v(0)=u\).
(i) Find \(v(t)\), and show the particle never stops in finite time.
(ii) Using \(v\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dx}=-kv\), find the total distance it travels.
(iii) Now suppose instead \(\dot v=-kv^2\). Show that the particle again never stops — but this time travels an infinite distance. Reconcile the two answers physically.

Show hints

(i) The separable equation of the Differentiation topic: \(v=ue^{-kt}>0\) always. (ii) \(\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dx}=-k\), so \(v=u-kx\): the speed hits zero at \(x=\frac uk\) — total distance \(\frac uk\), approached asymptotically. (iii) For \(v^{-2}\,\mathrm dv=-k\,\mathrm dt\): \(v=\frac u{1+ukt}\); and \(\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dx}=-kv\) gives \(v=ue^{-kx}\), positive for all \(x\) — the quadratic drag dies away so fast at low speed that it can no longer bring the particle to rest within any bounded stretch of road.
