Topic 3 · Sequences and Series

Sequences

A bridge between Algebra and Calculus: recurrences, arithmetic and geometric progressions, convergence and periodicity.

§4.1Introduction

Before jumping into Analysis, we take a short chapter on one of the bridges between Algebra and Calculus: sequences. We study them separately because they use very varied ideas, and the material on them is vast — admission test syllabuses treat them as a topic of their own.

§4.2Basic properties

Definition — Sequence

A sequence is a function \(f:\mathbb N\to X\), where \(X\) is a set. We focus on real sequences, written \(a_{n\ge0}\), although most results generalise to \(\mathbb C\). A link between terms is called a recurrence relation; the most basic is the first-order linear one, \(a_{n+1}=ka_n+b\) for constants \(k,b\in\mathbb R\).

Before solving it, a lemma:

Geometric sums

Let \(k\in\mathbb R\setminus\{1\}\) and \(n\in\mathbb N\). Then \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}k^i=\frac{k^{n+1}-1}{k-1}.\)

Proof. Let \(S=\sum_{i=0}^nk^i\). Then \(kS=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}k^i\). Subtracting term by term, \((k-1)S=k^{n+1}-1\). ∎
First-order recurrence

Let \(a_{n\ge0}\) satisfy \(a_{n+1}=ka_n+b\), \(k\ne1\). Then \(\displaystyle a_{n+1}=k^{n+1}a_0+b\cdot\frac{k^{n+1}-1}{k-1}.\)

Idea of proof. Subtracting \(a_{n+1}=ka_n+b\) and \(a_n=ka_{n-1}+b\) gives \(a_{n+1}-a_n=k(a_n-a_{n-1})\), and inductively \(a_{n+1}-a_n=k^n(a_1-a_0)\). Summing these telescoping differences and using the geometric-sum lemma yields the formula. You don't need to memorise it — Mathematics isn't done by memorising, especially not the Mathematics needed at top universities. Understand the proof and re-derive it in exams. ∎

There are more exotic first-order recurrences, e.g. \(a_{n+1}=k_n\,a_n+b_n\) with varying coefficients. These require creativity rather than a general method — the problems below provide practice. Two first-order recurrences you must know:

Arithmetic sequence

\(a_{n+1}=a_n+r\) for a fixed \(r\in\mathbb R\) (the common difference). General term: \(a_n=a_0+nr\). Sum of the first \(n\) terms: \(\frac n2\big(2a_0+(n-1)r\big)\).

Geometric sequence

\(a_{n+1}=r\cdot a_n\) for a fixed \(r\) (the common ratio). General term: \(a_n=r^na_0\). Sums follow from the geometric-sum lemma above.

§4.3Higher order linear recurrences

Linear recurrence of order m

A sequence \(a_{n\ge0}\) such that \(a_{n+m}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}u_i\,a_{n+i}\), for some \(u_i\in\mathbb R\). Its characteristic equation is \(r^m-\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}u_i r^i=0\).

The characteristic equation has \(m\) roots, possibly repeated. If \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_d\) are the distinct roots with multiplicities \(b_1,\dots,b_d\), the general solution is a sum of terms \(c_{j,i}\,n^i\lambda_j^n\) for \(0\le i<b_j\), with the constants found from the first \(m\) terms of the sequence. You won't get high-order recurrences in admission tests; what matters is the second-order case:

Second order linear recurrences

Let \(a_{n+2}=u\,a_{n+1}+v\,a_n\), with characteristic equation \(\lambda^2-u\lambda-v=0\). The sequence is either of the form \[a_n=c_1\lambda_1^n+c_2\lambda_2^n\] (distinct roots \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\)), or \[a_n=c_1\lambda^n+c_2\,n\,\lambda^n\] (a unique repeated root \(\lambda\)). The roots may be complex; in that case the constants obtained from the first terms might be complex too — be careful with the calculations.

Example (Fibonacci). Find the general formula of the sequence \(F_{n+2}=F_{n+1}+F_n\). The characteristic equation \(\lambda^2-\lambda-1=0\) has solutions \(\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2\) (the golden ratio) and \(\frac{1-\sqrt5}2=-\varphi^{-1}\). It is left as an exercise to verify that, with \(F_0=0,F_1=1\), \[F_n=\frac{\varphi^n-(-\varphi)^{-n}}{\sqrt5}.\]

§4.4Convergence and periodicity

Convergence of sequences

The sequence \(a_n\) converges to a number \(l\) if, for any \(\epsilon>0\), there exists \(N\) such that \(|a_m-l|<\epsilon\) for any \(m>N\).

Will you use this definition? No — but talking about convergence without a proper definition isn't mathematically rigorous. Intuitively: a sequence converges to \(l\) if, as it goes on, it gets as close as we like to \(l\). The payoff is for geometric sums: what happens to \(\frac{k^{n+1}-1}{k-1}\) as \(n\to\infty\)? If \(|k|>1\), the power blows up — no convergence. If \(|k|<1\), \(k^n\to0\):

Convergence of geometric sums

If \(|k|<1\), then \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}k^i=\frac1{1-k}.\)

Property of limits

Let \(a_n\to l\) and let \(b_n\) be obtained from \(a_n\) by elementary operations (addition, multiplication, powers, exponentials, logarithms, or combinations thereof) in the form of a function \(f\). Then \(b_n\to f(l)\) as \(n\to\infty\).

Now look at \(a_n=2^n\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)\): the terms are \(2,1,2,1,\dots\) — no limit, but a repeating pattern. We have just discovered:

Periodic sequences

A sequence \(a_n\) is periodic if \(a_n=a_{n+p}\) for all \(n\); \(p\) is the period.

There isn't a smart way to determine periodicity, only intuition: sequences built from trigonometric functions or modular arithmetic are likely periodic. The only sure check is to compute a few terms.

§4.5Problems

Question 1STEP 1 1992

Let \(a_1=3\), \(a_{n+1}=3^{a_n}\) for \(n\ge1\) (thus \(a_2=3^3\), \(a_3=3^{3^3}\) and so on).
(i) What digit appears in the units place of \(a_7\)? (ii) Show that \(a_7\ge10^{100}\). (iii) What is \(\frac{2a_7+1}{a_7}\) correct to two decimal places? Justify your answer.

Question 2STEP 3 2015

If \(s_1,s_2,\dots\) and \(t_1,t_2,\dots\) are sequences of positive numbers, we write \((s_n)\le(t_n)\) to mean "there exists a positive integer \(m\) such that \(s_n\le t_n\) whenever \(n\ge m\)". Determine whether each of the following statements is true or false. For a true statement give a proof including an explicit determination of an appropriate \(m\); for a false statement give a counterexample.
(i) \((1000n)\le(n^2)\). (ii) If it is not the case that \((s_n)\le(t_n)\), then it is the case that \((t_n)\le(s_n)\). (iii) If \((s_n)\le(t_n)\) and \((t_n)\le(u_n)\), then \((s_n)\le(u_n)\). (iv) \((n^2)\le(2^n)\).

Question 3STEP 1 2002

I borrow \(C\) pounds at interest rate \(100\alpha\%\) per year. The interest is added at the end of each year; immediately after, I make a repayment. The amount I repay at the end of the \(k\)th year is \(R_k\) pounds and the amount I owe at the beginning of the \(k\)th year is \(C_k\) pounds (with \(C_1=C\)). Express \(C_{n+1}\) in terms of \(R_k\) \((k=1,\dots,n)\), \(\alpha\) and \(C\), and show that if I pay off the loan in \(N\) years with repayments \(R_k=(1+\alpha)^k r\) (\(r\) constant), then \(r=C/N\). If instead I pay off the loan in \(N\) equal repayments of \(R\) pounds, show that \[\frac RC=\frac{\alpha(1+\alpha)^N}{(1+\alpha)^N-1},\] and that \(R/C\approx27/103\) when \(\alpha=1/50\), \(N=4\).

Question 4STEP 2 2013

The following theorem may be used: if a sequence is bounded above and increasing, then it tends to a limit. The sequence \(u_1,u_2,\dots\) is defined by \(u_1=1\) and \[u_{n+1}=1+\frac1{u_n}\quad(n\ge1).\quad(*)\] (i) Show that, for \(n\ge3\), \(\displaystyle u_{n+2}-u_n=\frac{u_n-u_{n-2}}{(1+u_n)(1+u_{n-2})}\).
(ii) Prove, by induction or otherwise, that \(1\le u_n\le2\) for all \(n\).
(iii) Show that the sequence \(u_1,u_3,u_5,\dots\) tends to a limit, and that \(u_2,u_4,u_6,\dots\) tends to a limit. Find these limits and deduce that \(u_1,u_2,u_3,\dots\) tends to a limit. Would this conclusion change if the sequence were defined by (*) and \(u_1=3\)?

Question 5TMUA 2022

A selection \(S\) of \(n\) terms is taken from the arithmetic sequence \(1,4,7,10,\dots,70\). Consider the statement: (*) There are two distinct terms in \(S\) whose sum is 74. What is the smallest value of \(n\) for which (*) is necessarily true?

Question 6TMUA 2020

The 1st, 2nd and 3rd terms of a geometric progression are also the 1st, 4th and 6th terms, respectively, of an arithmetic progression. The sum to infinity of the geometric progression is 12. Find the 1st term of the geometric progression.

Question 7MAT 2020

The functions \(f(n)=2n+1\) and \(g(n)=4n\) are defined for positive integers. This question is about the set \(S\) of positive integers achievable by applying, in some order, a combination of \(f\)s and \(g\)s to the number 1. For example \(gfg(1)=g(f(4))=g(9)=36\) and \(ffgg(1)=67\), so 36 and 67 are in \(S\).
(i) Write out the binary expansion of 100.
(ii) Show that 100 is in \(S\) by describing explicitly a combination of \(f\)s and \(g\)s that achieves it.
(iii) Show that 200 is not in \(S\).
(iv) Show that if \(n\) is in \(S\), there is only one combination of \(f\)s and \(g\)s achieving \(n\).
(v) Let \(u_k\) be the number of elements of \(S\) in the range \(2^k\le n<2^{k+1}\). Show that \(u_{k+2}=u_{k+1}+u_k\) for \(k\ge0\).
(vi) Let \(s_k\) be the number of elements of \(S\) in the range \(1\le n<2^{k+1}\). Show that \(s_{k+2}=s_{k+1}+s_k+1\) for \(k\ge0\).

Question 8MAT 2019

For \(k\) a positive integer, define \(p_k(x)=(1+x)(1+x^2)(1+x^3)\cdots(1+x^k)=a_0+a_1x+\dots+a_Nx^N\).
(i) Write down the degree \(N\) of \(p_k(x)\) in terms of \(k\).
(ii) By setting \(x=1\), or otherwise, explain why \(a_{\max}\ge\dfrac{2^k}{N+1}\), where \(a_{\max}\) is the largest coefficient.
(iii) Fix \(i\ge0\). Explain why the value of \(a_i\) eventually becomes constant as \(k\) increases.
(iv) A student correctly computes \(p_6(x)\) and, on the basis of the calculation, guesses that \(a_i=a_{N-i}\) for \(0\le i\le N\). By substituting \(x^{-1}\) for \(x\), or otherwise, show the guess is correct for all positive integers \(k\).
(v) The student also guesses that all whole numbers in the range \(1,\dots,a_{\max}\) appear amongst the coefficients. Use part (ii) to show that this guess is wrong. Justify your answer.

Question 9BMO 1 1993 · Hard

For each positive integer \(c\), the sequence \(u_n\) of integers is defined by \[u_1=1,\quad u_2=c,\quad u_n=(2n+1)u_{n-1}-(n^2-1)u_{n-2}\quad(n\ge3).\] For which values of \(c\) does this sequence have the property that \(u_i\) divides \(u_j\) whenever \(i\le j\)?
