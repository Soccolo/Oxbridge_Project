Mathematical Logic and Proof

Half of the TMUA is not about computing but about reasoning: what statements mean, what follows from what, and how proofs go right — and wrong.

§L.1Introduction

Paper 2 of the TMUA is unlike anything in the A-level syllabus. The questions don't ask you to find a value; they ask whether an argument is valid, what a statement's negation is, whether a condition is necessary or sufficient, or which line of a "proof" contains the error. The mathematics inside the questions is deliberately easy — the logic around it is the exam. We met the first of these ideas in the Numbers topic, where induction taught us necessary versus sufficient; here we develop the full toolkit.

§L.2Statements and their negations

Statements

A statement is a sentence which is either true or false (never both, never neither). "\(7\) is prime" is a true statement; "\(\pi<3\)" is a false one; "\(x>2\)" is not a statement until we say what \(x\) is — it is a condition, which becomes a statement once quantified or instantiated.

Negation

The negation of a statement \(P\), written \(\lnot P\) ("not \(P\)"), is the statement which is true exactly when \(P\) is false. Negating twice returns the original: \(\lnot(\lnot P)=P\).

Negating sounds trivial and is the single richest source of Paper 2 traps. The traps:

Negation traps
  1. Inequalities: the negation of \(x>2\) is \(x\le2\), not \(x<2\). The negation must cover every case where the original fails — including equality.
  2. "And" and "or" swap (De Morgan's laws): \[\lnot(P\text{ and }Q)=(\lnot P)\text{ or }(\lnot Q),\qquad \lnot(P\text{ or }Q)=(\lnot P)\text{ and }(\lnot Q).\] "It is not true that \(n\) is even and positive" means "\(n\) is odd or \(n\le0\)". Note also that the mathematical "or" is inclusive: "\(P\) or \(Q\)" is true when both hold.
  3. Quantifiers swap: the negation of "for all \(x\), \(P(x)\)" is "there exists an \(x\) with \(\lnot P(x)\)" — and vice versa: \[\lnot\big(\forall x:P(x)\big)=\exists x:\lnot P(x),\qquad \lnot\big(\exists x:P(x)\big)=\forall x:\lnot P(x).\] To deny "every swan is white" you need just one black swan — a single counterexample disproves a universal claim. To deny "some swan is black" you must show every swan is non-black.

Quantifiers in slow motion

The two quantifiers

The symbol \(\forall\) means for every, while \(\exists\) means there exists at least one. A quantifier tells us how many values from a stated domain must make a condition true:

  • \(\forall x\in\mathbb R,\;x^2\ge0\) says that every real number has a non-negative square.
  • \(\exists x\in\mathbb R,\;x^2=4\) says that at least one real number has square 4. It does not say there is exactly one: both \(2\) and \(-2\) work.

The domain matters. The statement \(\exists x,\;x^2=2\) is false if \(x\) must be rational, but true if \(x\) may be real.

Order matters

When two quantifiers occur, read them from left to right. In general they cannot be swapped:

\[\forall x\,\exists y:\;y>x\qquad\text{and}\qquad \exists y\,\forall x:\;y>x.\]

The first statement is true over the real numbers: after somebody chooses \(x\), we may choose \(y=x+1\). The choice of \(y\) is allowed to depend on \(x\). The second is false: it asks for one fixed real number \(y\) that is greater than every real \(x\). Thus "everyone has someone who is taller" is very different from "there is someone taller than everyone."

Template — Negating quantified statements
  1. Write the statement with its quantifiers visible.
  2. Move from left to right, swapping each \(\forall\) with \(\exists\), and each \(\exists\) with \(\forall\).
  3. Negate the final condition: \(=\) becomes \(\ne\), \(>\) becomes \(\le\), "and" becomes "or", and so on.
  4. Keep the original domain. You are changing the claim, not the collection of objects being discussed.

In symbols:

\[\lnot\big(\forall x\,\exists y:\;P(x,y)\big) =\exists x\,\forall y:\;\lnot P(x,y).\]

A useful spoken version is: to refute "every \(x\) has a suitable \(y\)", find one \(x\) for which no \(y\) is suitable.

Worked example. Negate: "for every real \(x>0\) there exists a real \(y\) such that \(y^2<x\)." First expose the structure: \[\forall x>0\,\exists y\in\mathbb R:\;y^2<x.\] Swap \(\forall\) to \(\exists\), swap \(\exists\) to \(\forall\), and negate \(y^2<x\) to \(y^2\ge x\): \[\exists x>0\,\forall y\in\mathbb R:\;y^2\ge x.\] The restriction \(x>0\) still describes the domain of \(x\); it is not replaced by \(x\le0\).

Common quantifier mistakes
  • Proving a universal statement from examples: ten successful examples do not prove "for every"; one failed example disproves it.
  • Forgetting that a witness may depend on an earlier variable: in \(\forall x\,\exists y\), different values of \(x\) may use different values of \(y\).
  • Using a moving witness where one fixed witness is required: in \(\exists y\,\forall x\), the same \(y\) must work for every \(x\).
  • Reading "exists" as "exists exactly one": uniqueness needs its own words or the symbol \(\exists!\).

§L.3Implication, converse, contrapositive

Implication

"\(P\Rightarrow Q\)" ("if \(P\) then \(Q\)") asserts: whenever \(P\) is true, \(Q\) is true. It says nothing at all about what happens when \(P\) is false. Consequently \(P\Rightarrow Q\) is only false in one situation: \(P\) true and \(Q\) false. The statements "\(P\Rightarrow Q\)" and "\(\lnot P\) or \(Q\)" are identical.

From \(P\Rightarrow Q\) we can manufacture three related statements, and telling them apart is the heart of Paper 2:

The four faces of an implication
NameStatementRelation to the original
Original\(P\Rightarrow Q\)—
Converse\(Q\Rightarrow P\)independent — may be true or false regardless
Inverse\(\lnot P\Rightarrow\lnot Q\)equivalent to the converse
Contrapositive\(\lnot Q\Rightarrow\lnot P\)equivalent to the original

If both \(P\Rightarrow Q\) and \(Q\Rightarrow P\) hold, we write \(P\Leftrightarrow Q\): \(P\) if and only if ("iff") \(Q\); the statements are equivalent.

Example. "If \(n\) is a multiple of 4, then \(n\) is even" — true. Converse: "if \(n\) is even, then \(n\) is a multiple of 4" — false (\(n=6\)). Contrapositive: "if \(n\) is odd, then \(n\) is not a multiple of 4" — true, automatically, because the contrapositive of a true statement is always true. The most common Paper 2 error is treating an implication and its converse as the same statement — in everyday speech "if" often smuggles in "only if", but in mathematics never.

§L.4Necessary and sufficient

These words are precise synonyms for the directions of implication:

Translation table
  1. "\(P\) is sufficient for \(Q\)" means \(P\Rightarrow Q\): having \(P\) is enough to guarantee \(Q\).
  2. "\(P\) is necessary for \(Q\)" means \(Q\Rightarrow P\): you cannot have \(Q\) without \(P\).
  3. "\(P\) is necessary and sufficient for \(Q\)" means \(P\Leftrightarrow Q\).
  4. "\(Q\) if \(P\)" means \(P\Rightarrow Q\); "\(Q\) only if \(P\)" means \(Q\Rightarrow P\).

Example. For a real number \(x\): "\(x>3\)" is sufficient for "\(x^2>4\)" (it forces it) but not necessary (\(x=-5\) works too). "\(x\ne2\)" is necessary for "\(x^2\ne4\)" but not sufficient (\(x=-2\) fails). And as we saw with induction in the Numbers topic: in weak induction, the truth of \(P(k)\) is both necessary and sufficient for the inductive step to deliver \(P(k+1)\); in strong induction it is necessary but not sufficient.

A reasoning checklist for "must it be true?" questions

Many TMUA questions give conditions and ask which statements must follow. The discipline:

  1. To show a statement must be true, derive it from the given conditions for every allowed case.
  2. To show it needn't be true, exhibit one concrete counterexample satisfying all the givens.
  3. Beware reasoning from a special case: checking one convenient example can only ever disprove, never prove, a universal claim.

§L.5The dictionary of statements

What follows comes straight from my tutoring sessions: a compact "dictionary" for translating exam English into implications, fast. In Paper 2 you have under two minutes per question; the dictionary makes the translation mechanical, so your thinking time is spent on the mathematics rather than on the wording. Throughout, \(A\) and \(B\) are mathematical sentences — for instance \(A\): "\(x\) is a multiple of 3" and \(B\): "\(x\) is a multiple of 6".

If/then statements
  1. If \(A\) then \(B\):  \(A\Rightarrow B\).
  2. \(A\) if \(B\):  \(B\Rightarrow A\).
  3. \(A\) only if \(B\):  \(A\Rightarrow B\).  ("\(x\) is a multiple of 6 only if \(x\) is a multiple of 3.")
  4. \(A\) if and only if \(B\):  \(A\Rightarrow B\) and \(B\Rightarrow A\).
Necessity and sufficiency
  1. \(A\) is necessary for \(B\):  \(B\Rightarrow A\).  ("\(x\) being a multiple of 3 is necessary for \(x\) to be a multiple of 6.")
  2. \(A\) is sufficient for \(B\):  \(A\Rightarrow B\).
Tip

A statement \(A\) is sufficient for a statement \(B\) if, with only the knowledge from statement \(A\), you can prove the entirety of statement \(B\). Statement \(A\) may contain more information than needed for statement \(B\), so it may not be necessary. For instance, with \(A\): "\(x\) is a multiple of 12" and \(B\): "\(x\) is a multiple of 6" — the fact that \(x\) is a multiple of 12 is sufficient to prove that \(x\) is a multiple of 6, but it contains more information than needed, because not every multiple of 6 must be a multiple of 12.

The dictionary entries chain together — these identifications are worth reciting until they are reflexes:

The grand identifications
  1. "If \(A\) then \(B\)" \(=\) "\(A\) only if \(B\)" \(=\) "\(A\) is sufficient for \(B\)" — all three are \(A\Rightarrow B\).
  2. "\(A\) if \(B\)" \(=\) "\(A\) is necessary for \(B\)" — both are \(B\Rightarrow A\).
  3. If \(A\) is necessary for \(B\), then \(B\) is sufficient for \(A\) — the same arrow read from the other end.
Template — Testing sufficiency and necessity by counterexample
  1. To check whether \(C\) is sufficient for \(B\), you have to see whether \(C\Rightarrow B\). Look for a counterexample: if statement \(B\) isn't true for a number that satisfies statement \(C\), then \(C\) is not sufficient for \(B\). For instance, \(C\): "\(x\) is a multiple of 4", \(B\): "\(x\) is a multiple of 6": \(x=4\) is a multiple of 4 but not of 6, therefore \(C\) is not sufficient for \(B\).
  2. Similarly, to check whether \(A\) is necessary for \(B\), look for a counterexample the other way: a number for which statement \(B\) is true but statement \(A\) isn't.
  3. No counterexample after honest searching is evidence, not proof — to establish sufficiency or necessity, prove the implication.
Template — "Exactly one statement is true"

Sometimes you may get a question where you have to find the true statement among many others, given that only one statement is true. In that case, rewrite all of the statements with the dictionary of statements and see which ones are the same. Equivalent statements share a truth value — so if several options translate to the same implication, none of them can be the unique true one, and the answer is the odd one out. Often you never need to think about the mathematics at all.

Drills from the tutoring room

A set of drills to get fluent with the dictionary of §L.5 before the harder problems — translate first, think second.

Drill 1Translation

Translate each into an implication (or a pair of them) and decide whether it is true. All variables are real unless stated otherwise.
(a) \(x\ge2\) if \(x\ge5\)
(b) \(x^2\ge4\) only if \(x\ge2\)
(c) an integer \(n\) is divisible by 6 if and only if it is divisible by both 2 and 3
(d) \(\dfrac1x>\dfrac1y\) if \(x<y\)
(e) \(x^3>8\) if and only if \(x>2\)
(f) a triangle is equilateral only if it is isosceles
(g) a triangle is isosceles if and only if it is equilateral

Show solutions

(a) True: reads \(x\ge5\Rightarrow x\ge2\).
(b) False: reads \(x^2\ge4\Rightarrow x\ge2\); counterexample \(x=-3\).
(c) True: both directions hold — it is the prime-factor criterion for divisibility by 6.
(d) False: reads \(x<y\Rightarrow\frac1x>\frac1y\), which holds only when \(x,y\) share a sign; take \(x=-1,\;y=1\), where \(x<y\) yet \(\frac1x=-1<1=\frac1y\).
(e) True: \(t\mapsto t^3\) is strictly increasing, so \(x^3>8\Leftrightarrow x>2\) — contrast (b), where the even power broke one direction.
(f) True: reads (equilateral) \(\Rightarrow\) (isosceles).
(g) False: the forward direction (isosceles \(\Rightarrow\) equilateral) fails — an isosceles triangle need not have all three sides equal.

Drill 2Classification

For each pair, decide whether \(P\) is necessary, sufficient, both, or neither, for \(Q\):
(a) \(P\): \(x=3\);   \(Q\): \(x^2=9\).
(b) \(P\): an integer is even;   \(Q\): an integer is divisible by 4.
(c) \(P\): an integer is divisible by 9;   \(Q\): the digit sum of the integer is divisible by 9.
(d) \(P\): \(x>0\);   \(Q\): \(x^2>x\).
(e) \(P\): \(\sin\theta=1\);   \(Q\): \(\cos\theta=0\).

Show solutions

(a) Sufficient, not necessary: \(x=3\Rightarrow x^2=9\), but \(x=-3\) gives \(Q\) without \(P\).
(b) Necessary, not sufficient: \(Q\Rightarrow P\) (multiples of 4 are even), yet \(x=6\) is even without being a multiple of 4.
(c) Both: the divisibility-by-9 test makes \(P\Leftrightarrow Q\).
(d) Neither: \(x^2>x\) holds exactly for \(x<0\) or \(x>1\); \(x=\tfrac12\) gives \(P\) without \(Q\), and \(x=-1\) gives \(Q\) without \(P\).
(e) Sufficient, not necessary: \(\sin\theta=1\Rightarrow\theta=\tfrac\pi2+2k\pi\Rightarrow\cos\theta=0\); but \(\theta=\tfrac{3\pi}2\) gives \(\cos\theta=0\) with \(\sin\theta=-1\).

Drill 3Exactly one is true

(a) \(a,b\) are real numbers and \(h\) is a function. Given that exactly one of the following is true, which one is it?
A. If \(a^2=b^2\) then \(h(a)=h(b)\)   B. \(a^2=b^2\) only if \(h(a)=h(b)\)   C. \(h(a)=h(b)\) is necessary for \(a^2=b^2\)   D. \(h(a)=h(b)\) is sufficient for \(a^2=b^2\)

(b) \(n\) is a positive integer. Given that exactly one of the following is true, which one is it?
A. \(n\) being even is necessary for \(n\) being divisible by 4   B. If \(n\) is even then \(n\) is divisible by 4   C. \(n\) is even only if \(n\) is divisible by 4   D. \(n\) being divisible by 4 is necessary for \(n\) being even

(c) \(x\) is a real number. Given that exactly one of the following is true, which one is it?
A. \(x^2>0\) only if \(x>0\)   B. If \(x^2>0\) then \(x>0\)   C. \(x>0\) is sufficient for \(x^2>0\)   D. \(x>0\) is necessary for \(x^2>0\)   E. \(x^2>0\) if and only if \(x>0\)

Show solutions

(a) D. A, B and C all translate to \(a^2=b^2\Rightarrow h(a)=h(b)\) — three copies of one statement, which must share a truth value, so they cannot be the unique true one. The odd one out is D (\(h(a)=h(b)\Rightarrow a^2=b^2\)), and pure logic forces it to be the answer — no facts about \(h\) required.
(b) A. B, C and D each read "\(n\) even \(\Rightarrow n\) divisible by 4", which is false (take \(n=2\)). The odd one out, A, reads "\(n\) divisible by 4 \(\Rightarrow n\) even" — true. ✓
(c) C. A, B and D each say "\(x^2>0\Rightarrow x>0\)", false (take \(x=-1\)); E is the two-way version, also false. C alone says "\(x>0\Rightarrow x^2>0\)", which is true. ✓

Drill 4TMUA-style

The units digit of \(7^n\) (for positive integers \(n\)) repeats in a cycle. Which of the following is a sufficient but not necessary condition for \(7^n\) to end in the digit \(1\)?
A. \(n\) is a multiple of 4   B. \(n\) is a multiple of 8   C. \(n\) is even   D. \(n\equiv2\pmod4\)   E. \(n\ge4\)   F. \(n\) is a multiple of 4 or of 6

Show solution

B. The units digits of \(7^1,7^2,7^3,7^4,\dots\) run \(7,9,3,1,7,9,3,1,\dots\) — period 4 — so \(7^n\) ends in 1 exactly when \(n\equiv0\pmod4\). Now test each option's set against the solution set \(\{n:4\mid n\}\): a sufficient condition is a subset of it, and not necessary means a strict subset. A is the solution set itself — sufficient but also necessary, so it fails "not necessary". B (multiples of 8) is a strict subset: every multiple of 8 is a multiple of 4 (sufficient ✓), yet \(n=4\) is missed (not necessary ✓). C, D, E and F each contain a value with \(n\not\equiv0\pmod4\) — namely \(n=2,\,2,\,5,\,6\) — so none of them even guarantees the digit, i.e. none is sufficient. The exam move is always the same: pin down the solution set exactly, then test each option for containment.

Drill 5TMUA-style

Let \(f\) be a polynomial. Which of the following is necessary for the statement \[\frac{f(x)+f(y)}{2}\ge f\!\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\qquad\text{for all real }x\text{ and }y?\] A. \(f\ge0\)   B. \(f'\ge0\)   C. \(f''\ge0\)   D. \(f\le0\)   E. \(f'\le0\)   F. \(f''\le0\)

Show solution 1 — Jensen's inequality
Jensen's inequality

A function is called convex when its graph bends upwards: the straight chord joining any two points on the graph lies on or above the graph. Jensen's inequality says that for a convex function \(f\), a weighted average of the function values is at least the function evaluated at the same weighted average of the inputs:

\[\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)\ge f\big(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\big),\qquad 0\le\lambda\le1.\]

With equal weights \(\lambda=\tfrac12\), this becomes \[\frac{f(x)+f(y)}2\ge f\!\left(\frac{x+y}2\right),\] which is exactly the statement in the question. For a twice-differentiable function, and therefore for a polynomial, convexity is equivalent to \(f''(t)\ge0\) for every real \(t\). Hence the necessary condition is C: \(f''\ge0\).

The other signs are not necessary. For example, adding a constant changes whether \(f\) is positive or negative without changing the inequality; a convex polynomial can also decrease on part of its domain and increase on another part, as \(f(x)=x^2\) does.

Show solution 2 — Start with the smallest non-trivial polynomial

A constant or linear polynomial has \(f''=0\), so its midpoint inequality is always an equality and reveals no sign. The smallest degree with a non-trivial second derivative is therefore two. Set \[f(x)=ax^2+bx+c.\] Then \[\frac{f(x)+f(y)}2-f\!\left(\frac{x+y}2\right)=\frac{a}{4}(x-y)^2.\] For this to be non-negative for all real \(x,y\), we must have \(a\ge0\). Since \(f''=2a\), the quadratic case points precisely to \(f''\ge0\).

Now make the argument valid for an arbitrary polynomial. Fix any real number \(t\), and choose \(x=t+h\) and \(y=t-h\). Their midpoint is \(t\), so the given statement says \[f(t+h)-2f(t)+f(t-h)\ge0.\] For \(h\ne0\), divide by \(h^2>0\): \[\frac{f(t+h)-2f(t)+f(t-h)}{h^2}\ge0.\] As \(h\to0\), the left-hand side tends to \(f''(t)\). Therefore \(f''(t)\ge0\). Because \(t\) was arbitrary, \(f''\ge0\) for every real input. The answer is again C.

§L.6Methods of proof

We collected proof techniques throughout the book; here is the full arsenal in one place.

The arsenal
  1. Direct proof: assume \(P\), deduce \(Q\) by a chain of implications.
  2. Proof by exhaustion: split into finitely many cases and verify each — how we handled residues in modular arithmetic (squares mod 4: check \(n\equiv0,1,2,3\) separately).
  3. Proof by contrapositive: to prove \(P\Rightarrow Q\), prove \(\lnot Q\Rightarrow\lnot P\) instead — they are equivalent, and the contrapositive is often easier. To show "if \(n^2\) is even then \(n\) is even", show "if \(n\) is odd then \(n^2\) is odd": \(n=2k+1\) gives \(n^2=2(2k^2+2k)+1\). Done.
  4. Proof by contradiction (reduction to absurd): assume the statement is false and derive an impossibility — the template from the Numbers topic, including its specialised form, infinite descent, with which we proved \(\sqrt2\) irrational.
  5. Proof by induction (weak and strong): the templates of §2.2.
  6. Disproof by counterexample: one example where the claim fails. A counterexample must satisfy all the hypotheses and violate the conclusion.

§L.7Identifying errors in proofs

The signature Paper 2 question: a purported proof with numbered lines, one of which is rotten. The classic rots, ranked by frequency:

The rogues' gallery
  1. Dividing by something that may be zero. The flagship fake proof that \(1=2\): let \(a=b\); then \(a^2=ab\); then \(a^2-b^2=ab-b^2\); factor: \((a-b)(a+b)=b(a-b)\); divide by \(a-b\) to get \(a+b=b\), i.e. \(2b=b\), so \(2=1\). The division by \(a-b=0\) is the crime. The same crime in trigonometric disguise: cancelling \(\cos\theta\) in Question 8 of the Geometry topic.
  2. Squaring (or applying any non-injective operation) and keeping all the "solutions". From \(x=\sqrt{x+2}\), squaring gives \(x^2-x-2=0\), so \(x=2\) or \(x=-1\) — but \(x=-1\) fails the original (a square root is non-negative). Squaring is a valid forward implication, not an equivalence; every solution of the original solves the squared equation, but not conversely. Always substitute candidates back.
  3. Multiplying or dividing an inequality by a quantity of unknown sign. From \(\frac1x<1\) it does not follow that \(1<x\): if \(x<0\) the multiplication flips the sign (recall Remark 2 of the Algebra topic), and indeed every negative \(x\) satisfies \(\frac1x<1\).
  4. Assuming the converse / affirming the consequent. Knowing \(P\Rightarrow Q\) and observing \(Q\), one cannot conclude \(P\).
  5. Circular reasoning: using (a disguised form of) the statement being proved as a step in its own proof.
  6. An induction whose step fails for small \(n\). The famous "all horses are the same colour" proof breaks because the inductive step requires two overlapping sets, which fails going from \(n=1\) to \(n=2\). When checking an induction, test the step at its smallest instance.
  7. Taking square roots carelessly: \(x^2=y^2\) gives \(|x|=|y|\), i.e. \(x=\pm y\) — not \(x=y\).
Template — Auditing a proof
  1. Read the conclusion first; if it is absurd (\(1=2\)), an error certainly exists — your job is to locate the first invalid line, not merely a suspicious one.
  2. For each line, ask: is this step an equivalence (\(\Leftrightarrow\)) or a one-way implication (\(\Rightarrow\))? One-way steps can introduce false solutions (squaring) or lose true ones (dividing).
  3. Test each division, cancellation, root and inequality manipulation against zero and against negative values.
  4. If the proof is an induction, verify the base case and run the inductive step by hand at the smallest \(k\).

§L.8Problems

Question 1New · Paper 2 style

Write down the negation of each statement:
(i) Every prime greater than 2 is odd.
(ii) There exists a real \(x\) with \(x^2+1=0\).
(iii) \(n\) is positive and even.
(iv) For every \(\epsilon>0\) there exists \(N\) such that for all \(n>N\), \(|a_n|<\epsilon\).

Show solution

(i) There exists a prime greater than 2 which is even. (ii) For every real \(x\), \(x^2+1\ne0\). (iii) \(n\) is non-positive or odd (De Morgan; note \(\le0\), not \(<0\)). (iv) There exists \(\epsilon>0\) such that for every \(N\) there exists \(n>N\) with \(|a_n|\ge\epsilon\) — flip every quantifier in turn, negate the core, and \(<\) becomes \(\ge\). (You have just negated the definition of a sequence converging to 0.)

Question 2New · Paper 2 style

For real numbers \(x\), consider \(P\): "\(x^2-5x+6=0\)" and \(Q\): "\(x=2\)". Which of the following is true?
A. \(P\) is necessary and sufficient for \(Q\).   B. \(P\) is necessary but not sufficient for \(Q\).   C. \(P\) is sufficient but not necessary for \(Q\).   D. \(P\) is neither.

Show solution

B. \(P\) holds iff \(x=2\) or \(x=3\). \(Q\Rightarrow P\) (so \(P\) is necessary for \(Q\)); but \(P\not\Rightarrow Q\), as \(x=3\) satisfies \(P\) and not \(Q\) (so \(P\) is not sufficient).

Question 3New · Paper 2 style

State the converse and the contrapositive of: "If a quadrilateral is a square, then it has equal diagonals." Decide the truth of all three statements.

Show solution

Original: true. Converse: "if a quadrilateral has equal diagonals, then it is a square" — false (any non-square rectangle, or an isosceles trapezium). Contrapositive: "if a quadrilateral does not have equal diagonals, then it is not a square" — true, automatically equivalent to the original.

Question 4New · Paper 2 style

The integers \(a,b\) satisfy \(ab>0\) and \(a+b>0\). Which of the following must be true?
I. \(a>0\)   II. \(b>0\)   III. \(a^2+b^2>0\)

Show solution

All three. \(ab>0\) forces \(a,b\) to share a sign; \(a+b>0\) rules out both negative; hence both are positive (I and II), and III follows since neither is zero. The exam-day discipline: try to break each statement with signed examples before concluding "must".

Question 5New · Paper 2 style

A student "proves" that \(-2=2\):
(1) \((-2)^2=4\).   (2) \(2^2=4\).   (3) Therefore \((-2)^2=2^2\).   (4) Taking square roots of both sides, \(-2=2\).
In which line is the error, and what exactly is wrong?

Show solution

Line (4). From \(x^2=y^2\) one may conclude only \(|x|=|y|\), i.e. \(x=\pm y\). "Taking the square root of both sides" of an equality of squares is a non-injective step — rogue #7 in our gallery.

Question 6New · Paper 2 style

A student solves \(\sqrt{x+3}=x-3\):
(1) Squaring: \(x+3=x^2-6x+9\).   (2) So \(x^2-7x+6=0\).   (3) Factorising: \((x-1)(x-6)=0\).   (4) Hence the solutions are \(x=1\) and \(x=6\).
Identify and explain the error, and give the correct solution set.

Show solution

Lines (1)–(3) are valid one-way implications; the error is the word "hence" in line (4), which silently upgrades \(\Rightarrow\) to \(\Leftrightarrow\). Squaring can introduce solutions: checking, \(x=1\) gives \(\sqrt4=2\) but \(x-3=-2\) — fails; \(x=6\) gives \(\sqrt9=3=6-3\) — works. The solution set is \(\{6\}\).

Question 7New · Paper 2 style

Prove, by the method of your choice, that if \(n^2+2\) is divisible by 3, then \(n\) is not divisible by 3. Which proof method did you use, and what are the contrapositive and converse of the statement? Is the converse true?

Show solution

Contrapositive: "if \(n\) is divisible by 3, then \(n^2+2\) is not divisible by 3" — and this is immediate: \(n=3k\) gives \(n^2+2=9k^2+2\equiv2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)\). Proof by contrapositive. Converse: "if \(n\) is not divisible by 3, then \(n^2+2\) is divisible by 3" — true as well! If \(n\equiv\pm1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)\) then \(n^2\equiv1\), so \(n^2+2\equiv0\). The original is thus an "iff" in disguise, but proving the converse required its own argument — the truth of an implication never comes free with its converse.

Question 8New · Paper 2 style

Consider the claim: "for all positive integers \(n\), \(n^2+n+41\) is prime." A student verifies it for \(n=1,2,\dots,20\) and declares it proven. What is wrong with the argument, and what is the status of the claim?

Show solution

Twenty verifications prove nothing about "for all" — a universal claim needs a general argument; examples can only disprove. And the claim is in fact false: \(n=41\) gives \(41^2+41+41=41\cdot43\), composite (no computation needed — every term is divisible by 41). Euler's famous polynomial produces primes for \(n=1,\dots,39\) and then betrays you: the danger of pattern-spotting without proof.

Question 9New · Paper 2 style

Here is a "proof" that every real number equals 0. "Let \(x\) be any real number and let \(S=x+x^2+x^3+\dots\) Then \(xS=x^2+x^3+\dots=S-x\), so \(S(x-1)=-x\), giving \(S=\frac{x}{1-x}\), which is finite. But if \(x\ge1\) the sum is clearly infinite, a contradiction — unless \(x=0\)." Locate the error.

Show solution

The first line. Writing \(S=x+x^2+\dots\) and manipulating it presumes the infinite sum converges — which, as we saw in the Sequences topic, holds only for \(|x|<1\). For \(|x|\ge1\), \(S\) is not a number, and the algebra performed on it is meaningless. Manipulating a divergent series as if it were a number is a subtler cousin of dividing by zero.

Question 10New · Paper 2 style

The statement \(P(n)\) satisfies: \(P(n)\Rightarrow P(n+2)\) for every positive integer \(n\); \(P(4)\) is true; and \(P(7)\) is false. For which \(n\) can the truth value of \(P(n)\) be determined, and what is it?

Show solution

From \(P(4)\) true and the step: \(P(n)\) is true for all even \(n\ge4\). From \(P(7)\) false, by the contrapositive \(\lnot P(n+2)\Rightarrow\lnot P(n)\): \(P(5)\), \(P(3)\) and \(P(1)\) are all false. Determined: even \(n\ge4\) (true) and odd \(n\le7\) (false). Undetermined: \(n=2\) (the implication only pushes forward) and odd \(n\ge9\) (a false hypothesis implies nothing). This is induction's machinery viewed through pure logic — the step transmits truth forwards and falsehood backwards, never the reverse.

Question 11TMUA 2020 (see Algebra Q9)

The real numbers \(a,b,c,d\) satisfy both \(0<a+b<c+d\) and \(0<a+c<b+d\). Which of the following must be true?
I. \(a<d\)   II. \(b<c\)   III. \(a+b+c+d>0\)
(Revisit this genuine TMUA question, from the Algebra problems, with the checklist of §L.4 in hand.)

Show solution

Adding the two given inequalities: \(2a+b+c<b+c+2d\), so \(a<d\) — I must be true. III: \(a+b>0\) and... careful — add \(0<a+b\) and \(0<c+d\)? We only know \(a+b<c+d\) and \(a+b>0\), hence \(c+d>0\) too, and the total is positive — III must be true. II: try to break it. Take \(a=0,b=2,c=1,d=4\): then \(a+b=2<5=c+d\) ✓, \(a+c=1<6=b+d\) ✓, but \(b=2>1=c\) — II fails. Answer: I and III only.

Question 12New · Paper 2 style · Hard

Three statements about a function \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\):
\(P\): \(f(x)>0\) for all \(x\).   \(Q\): \(f(x)\ne0\) for all \(x\).   \(R\): there is no \(x\) with \(f(x)\le0\).
Which pairs of statements are equivalent? For each non-equivalent pair, state which implication holds and give a counterexample to the other.

Show solution

\(P\) and \(R\) are equivalent: "no \(x\) with \(f(x)\le0\)" is exactly the negation-of-existence form of "for all \(x\), \(f(x)>0\)". \(P\Rightarrow Q\) (positive numbers aren't zero) but \(Q\not\Rightarrow P\): \(f(x)=-1\) satisfies \(Q\) and violates \(P\). So \(P\Leftrightarrow R\), and \(Q\) is strictly weaker. The deeper lesson: a "for all" statement and the non-existence of a counterexample are the same statement in different clothing — the quantifier laws of §L.2.
