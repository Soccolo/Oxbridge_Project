Statistics

Conditional probability, random variables, the normal distribution and its approximations, and the chi-squared test. Personally, as an actuary and Statistics enjoyer, I find the statistics questions easier than the pure ones — by the end of this topic, you may agree.

§S.1Introduction

The other two unpopular questions on every STEP paper are statistics, and the same leverage argument from the Mechanics topic applies. STEP statistics is really probability with calculus: the counting of the Counting topic, conditional reasoning, and then random variables — whose continuous version is an application of the Integration topic, full stop. There is very little to memorise and a great deal to set up carefully.

§S.2Conditional probability

Conditional probability

The probability of \(A\) given that \(B\) has occurred: \[\mathbb P(A\mid B)=\frac{\mathbb P(A\text{ and }B)}{\mathbb P(B)}\qquad(\mathbb P(B)>0).\] Rearranged, the multiplication law: \(\mathbb P(A\text{ and }B)=\mathbb P(B)\,\mathbb P(A\mid B)\) — the rule that tree diagrams implement branch by branch. Events are independent precisely when \(\mathbb P(A\mid B)=\mathbb P(A)\): knowing \(B\) tells you nothing about \(A\).

Total probability and Bayes

If \(B_1,\dots,B_n\) are mutually exclusive and cover everything, then \[\mathbb P(A)=\sum_i\mathbb P(B_i)\,\mathbb P(A\mid B_i)\] (law of total probability — sum over the branches of the tree), and the conditioning can be reversed: \[\mathbb P(B_k\mid A)=\frac{\mathbb P(B_k)\,\mathbb P(A\mid B_k)}{\sum_i\mathbb P(B_i)\,\mathbb P(A\mid B_i)}\] (Bayes' theorem — nothing more than the definition applied twice, but the engine of every "given the test is positive…" question).

The two classic confusions
  1. \(\mathbb P(A\mid B)\ne\mathbb P(B\mid A)\). The probability of a positive test given the disease is not the probability of the disease given a positive test — the whole point of Bayes.
  2. "Mutually exclusive" and "independent" are near-opposites, not synonyms: exclusive events with positive probabilities cannot be independent, since one occurring forbids the other.

Example. A disease affects 1% of a population; a test detects it with probability 0.95, but also flags 5% of healthy people. Given a positive test, the probability of disease is \[\frac{0.01\times0.95}{0.01\times0.95+0.99\times0.05}=\frac{0.0095}{0.0590}\approx0.16,\] not 0.95 — the false positives from the huge healthy majority swamp the true ones. STEP loves this structure precisely because the answer offends intuition and rewards the mechanical tree.

§S.3Discrete random variables

Random variables, expectation, variance

A discrete random variable \(X\) takes values \(x_i\) with probabilities \(p_i\) summing to 1. Its expectation (mean) and variance are \[\mathbb E(X)=\sum_ix_ip_i,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E\!\left((X-\mu)^2\right)=\mathbb E(X^2)-\big(\mathbb E(X)\big)^2,\] with \(\mu=\mathbb E(X)\); the second form of the variance is the one you compute with. The standard deviation is \(\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}\).

The algebra of expectation
  1. \(\mathbb E(aX+b)=a\,\mathbb E(X)+b\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\) — shifting doesn't change spread; scaling scales it quadratically.
  2. \(\mathbb E(X+Y)=\mathbb E(X)+\mathbb E(Y)\) always — linearity holds with no independence assumption, which is what makes it the most powerful cheap trick in the subject.
  3. \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)\) only for independent \(X,Y\).
The standard distributions
  1. Binomial \(B(n,p)\): the number of successes in \(n\) independent trials with success probability \(p\): \[\mathbb P(X=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad\mathbb E(X)=np,\quad\operatorname{Var}(X)=np(1-p).\] The probabilities are the terms of \((p+q)^n\) with \(q=1-p\) — the Binomial Theorem earning its name.
  2. Geometric: the trial number of the first success: \(\mathbb P(X=k)=q^{k-1}p\), with \(\mathbb E(X)=\frac1p\) — provable with the geometric series of the Sequences topic, or by the elegant self-similarity argument: \(\mathbb E(X)=1+q\,\mathbb E(X)\) (one trial spent; with probability \(q\) you are back where you started).

Example. A die is rolled until the first six. Expected number of rolls: \(\frac1{1/6}=6\). And \(\mathbb P(\text{more than }k\text{ rolls})=\left(\frac56\right)^k\) — "more than \(k\)" means \(k\) initial failures, a complement worth internalising.

§S.4Continuous random variables

When \(X\) can take any value in an interval, probabilities are no longer attached to points but spread as a density — and everything becomes the Integration topic:

Probability density and cumulative distribution

A probability density function (pdf) \(f\) satisfies \(f(x)\ge0\) and \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mathrm dx=1\), and \[\mathbb P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^bf(x)\,\mathrm dx\] — probability is area under the density. Note \(\mathbb P(X=a)=0\) for any single point: only intervals carry probability. The cumulative distribution function (cdf) is \[F(x)=\mathbb P(X\le x)=\int_{-\infty}^xf(t)\,\mathrm dt,\] so \(F'=f\): the cdf is the area-so-far function, and differentiating it recovers the density — the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus moonlighting as probability theory. The median \(m\) solves \(F(m)=\frac12\); quartiles solve \(F=\frac14,\frac34\).

Moments by integration

\[\mathbb E(X)=\int xf(x)\,\mathrm dx,\qquad\mathbb E(X^2)=\int x^2f(x)\,\mathrm dx,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E(X^2)-\mathbb E(X)^2,\] all integrals over the range where \(f\) is non-zero. For a density symmetric about \(x=c\), \(\mathbb E(X)=c\) with no computation — the odd-function symmetry of §7.5.

Template — A continuous random variable question
  1. If the density contains an unknown constant, fix it first: total area \(=1\).
  2. Compute whatever is asked — probabilities, mean, variance, median — as integrals; for piecewise densities, split the integrals at the joins.
  3. For the median and quartiles, find the cdf and solve \(F(m)=\frac12\); check which piece of a piecewise cdf the solution must land in before solving.
  4. For a transformed variable \(Y=g(X)\) (a STEP favourite), work through the cdf: \(F_Y(y)=\mathbb P(g(X)\le y)\), rewrite the event in terms of \(X\), then differentiate to get the new density.

Example. Let \(f(x)=kx(2-x)\) on \([0,2]\) (zero elsewhere). Then \(1=k\int_0^2(2x-x^2)\,\mathrm dx=k\left[x^2-\frac{x^3}3\right]_0^2=\frac43k\), so \(k=\frac34\). By symmetry about \(x=1\), \(\mathbb E(X)=1\) and the median is also 1. And \[\mathbb E(X^2)=\frac34\int_0^2\left(2x^3-x^4\right)\mathrm dx=\frac34\left(8-\frac{32}5\right)=\frac65,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac65-1=\frac15.\]

§S.5The normal distribution

One continuous distribution towers over the rest. The normal distribution \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) is the bell-shaped density centred at the mean \(\mu\) with standard deviation \(\sigma\) — symmetric, with tails that decay extremely fast. Its density \(f(x)=\frac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}\) cannot be integrated in closed form, which is why every normal calculation goes through tables (or a calculator) for the standard normal \(Z\sim N(0,1)\), whose cdf is written \(\Phi(z)=\mathbb P(Z\le z)\).

Template — Normal probabilities
  1. Standardise: if \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), then \(Z=\dfrac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1)\). Every question reduces to \(\Phi\) of something.
  2. Convert the event: \(\mathbb P(X\le x)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\).
  3. Handle tails and intervals with the two symmetry rules: \(\mathbb P(Z>z)=1-\Phi(z)\) and \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\) — tables only list \(z\ge0\) because symmetry supplies the rest.
  4. Inverse problems ("find \(\mu\)", "find the cutoff"): translate the given probability into a \(z\)-value first (e.g. \(\Phi(1.96)=0.975\)), then unwind \(x=\mu+z\sigma\). With two unknown parameters, two conditions give simultaneous equations in \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\).

Values worth knowing by heart, both for speed and for sanity-checking: \(\Phi(1)\approx0.8413\), \(\Phi(2)\approx0.9772\), \(\Phi(1.645)\approx0.95\), \(\Phi(1.96)\approx0.975\) — and the 68–95–99.7 rule: roughly 68% of the distribution lies within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two, 99.7% within three.

Example. IQ scores are modelled as \(X\sim N(100,15^2)\). Then \[\mathbb P(X>115)=\mathbb P\!\left(Z>\tfrac{115-100}{15}\right)=\mathbb P(Z>1)=1-\Phi(1)\approx0.159.\] And the score exceeded by only 2.5% of the population solves \(\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-100}{15}\right)=0.975\): \(x=100+1.96\cdot15\approx129\).

§S.6Approximating with the normal

The normal distribution's real power is as an approximation: sums of many small independent contributions are approximately normal (the Central Limit Theorem, which you will meet properly at university). The exam-level instance is the binomial. A \(B(n,p)\) count is a sum of \(n\) independent 0/1 trials, so for large \(n\) its bar chart looks like a bell:

Normal approximation to the binomial

If \(X\sim B(n,p)\) with \(n\) large and \(p\) not too extreme (rule of thumb: \(np>5\) and \(n(1-p)>5\)), then approximately \[X\;\approx\;N\big(np,\;np(1-p)\big)\] — same mean, same variance, as it must be. Because a discrete count is being replaced by a continuous density, apply the continuity correction: the discrete event \(X\ge k\) becomes the continuous event \(X>k-\tfrac12\), and \(X\le k\) becomes \(X<k+\tfrac12\) — each integer \(k\) owns the interval \(\left[k-\tfrac12,k+\tfrac12\right]\).

Example. A fair coin is tossed 100 times; estimate \(\mathbb P(X\ge60)\) heads. Here \(np=50\), \(np(1-p)=25\), so \(X\approx N(50,5^2)\), and with the continuity correction: \[\mathbb P(X\ge60)\approx\mathbb P\!\left(Z\ge\tfrac{59.5-50}{5}\right)=\mathbb P(Z\ge1.9)=1-\Phi(1.9)\approx1-0.9713=0.029.\] The exact binomial value is about 0.028 — the approximation, with its correction, is excellent. (Without the correction one gets \(\mathbb P(Z\ge2)\approx0.023\) — noticeably worse; in a multiple-choice setting the corrected and uncorrected values are often both offered as options.)

§S.7The chi-squared test

Beyond STEP now, into Further Statistics — and, I will admit as an actuary, into the single most-used test of my professional life. The question it answers: do observed counts fit a claimed model? Suppose a model predicts expected counts \(E_1,\dots,E_m\) across \(m\) categories, and an experiment delivers observed counts \(O_1,\dots,O_m\). The discrepancy is measured by \[\chi^2=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i},\] each category's squared error weighted by how large it was expected to be. If the model is right, this statistic follows (approximately) a chi-squared distribution, and a value beyond the tabulated critical value is evidence against the model.

Template — Goodness-of-fit test
  1. State the hypotheses: \(H_0\): the data follow the claimed model; \(H_1\): they do not.
  2. Compute the expected counts under \(H_0\) (total \(\times\) model probabilities). Every \(E_i\) should be at least 5 — merge small categories if not, since the approximation degrades.
  3. Compute \(\chi^2=\sum\frac{(O-E)^2}{E}\).
  4. Degrees of freedom: \(\nu=m-1\), minus one more for every parameter estimated from the data. For an \(r\times c\) contingency table (testing independence of two classifications, with expected counts \(E=\frac{\text{row total}\times\text{column total}}{\text{grand total}}\)): \(\nu=(r-1)(c-1)\).
  5. Compare with the critical value at the chosen significance level (5% values: \(\nu=1\): 3.841; \(\nu=2\): 5.991; \(\nu=3\): 7.815; \(\nu=4\): 9.488; \(\nu=5\): 11.070). Exceed it → reject \(H_0\); otherwise → no significant evidence against the model. Phrase the conclusion in context, and never claim the model is proved — failing to reject is not the same as confirming (a distinction straight out of the Logic topic).

Example. A die is rolled 60 times, giving observed counts \(5,8,9,8,10,20\) for the faces 1–6. Is the die fair, at the 5% level? Under fairness each \(E_i=10\), and \[\chi^2=\frac{25+4+1+4+0+100}{10}=13.4.\] With \(\nu=6-1=5\) the critical value is 11.07, and \(13.4>11.07\): reject \(H_0\) — significant evidence the die is unfair. Note where the statistic came from: the six 20s-worth of excess sits almost entirely in one cell, and the test duly noticed.

§S.8Problems

Question 1New

Two fair dice are rolled. Given that the total is at least 10, find the probability that at least one die shows a six. Are the events "total at least 10" and "at least one six" independent?

Show solution

Totals \(\ge10\): the six ordered pairs \((4,6),(5,5),(5,6),(6,4),(6,5),(6,6)\). Of these, five involve a six. \(\mathbb P=\frac56\). Independence would need \(\frac56=\mathbb P(\text{at least one six})=\frac{11}{36}\) — false, so not independent (obviously: high totals need high faces).

Question 2New · Bayes

Urn I contains 3 red and 1 blue ball; urn II contains 1 red and 3 blue. A fair coin chooses an urn, and a ball drawn from it turns out red. Find the probability the coin chose urn I. A second ball is then drawn from the same urn (without replacement): find the probability it is also red.

Show solution

Bayes: \(\mathbb P(\text{I}\mid R)=\frac{\frac12\cdot\frac34}{\frac12\cdot\frac34+\frac12\cdot\frac14}=\frac34\). Second draw, by total probability over the (updated) urn: \[\mathbb P(R_2\mid R_1)=\frac34\cdot\frac23+\frac14\cdot0=\frac12.\] Note the posterior \(\frac34\), not the prior \(\frac12\), feeds the second calculation — updating on evidence is the whole game.

Question 3New

The random variable \(X\) takes values \(1,2,3,4\) with probabilities proportional to \(\frac1k\). Find the distribution, \(\mathbb E(X)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\).

Show solution

Proportionality constant: \(c\left(1+\frac12+\frac13+\frac14\right)=1\) gives \(c=\frac{12}{25}\), so \(p_k=\frac{12}{25k}\). Then \(\mathbb E(X)=\sum k\cdot\frac{12}{25k}=\frac{48}{25}\), and \(\mathbb E(X^2)=\frac{12}{25}(1+2+3+4)=\frac{120}{25}=\frac{24}5\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{24}5-\left(\frac{48}{25}\right)^2=\frac{3000-2304}{625}=\frac{696}{625}\).

Question 4New · STEP-style

A coin with probability \(p\) of heads is tossed \(n\) times. Show that the probability of an even number of heads is \(\frac12\left(1+(1-2p)^n\right)\). (Hint: consider \((q+p)^n\) and \((q-p)^n\) — the paired-quantities trick of the Integration topic, transplanted.)

Show solution

Expand both: \((q+p)^n=\sum_k\binom nk p^kq^{n-k}=1\), and \((q-p)^n=\sum_k\binom nk(-p)^kq^{n-k}\), which carries \(+\) on even \(k\) and \(-\) on odd \(k\). Adding kills the odd terms and doubles the even ones: \[\mathbb P(\text{even})=\frac{(q+p)^n+(q-p)^n}2=\frac{1+(1-2p)^n}2.\] Sanity checks: \(p=\frac12\) gives \(\frac12\) for any \(n\); \(p=0\) gives 1 (zero heads is even).

Question 5New

The continuous random variable \(X\) has density \(f(x)=\frac3{x^4}\) for \(x\ge1\) (zero elsewhere). Verify it is a density; find \(\mathbb E(X)\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\) and the median. What goes wrong if the exponent 4 is replaced by 2?

Show solution

\(\int_1^\infty3x^{-4}\mathrm dx=\left[-x^{-3}\right]_1^\infty=1\) ✓. \(\mathbb E(X)=\int_1^\infty3x^{-3}\mathrm dx=\frac32\); \(\mathbb E(X^2)=\int_1^\infty3x^{-2}\mathrm dx=3\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=3-\frac94=\frac34\). Median: \(F(x)=1-x^{-3}=\frac12\) gives \(m=2^{1/3}\). With exponent 2, the density \(\frac1{x^2}\) still integrates to 1, but \(\mathbb E(X)=\int_1^\infty\frac{\mathrm dx}{x}\) diverges — a legitimate random variable with infinite mean, a standard STEP sting in the tail.

Question 6New · STEP-style

\(X\) is uniformly distributed on \([0,1]\) (density 1 there, 0 elsewhere) and \(Y=X^2\). Find the cdf and pdf of \(Y\), and compute \(\mathbb E(Y)\) twice: once from the density of \(Y\), and once directly as \(\mathbb E(X^2)\).

Show solution

For \(0\le y\le1\): \(F_Y(y)=\mathbb P(X^2\le y)=\mathbb P(X\le\sqrt y)=\sqrt y\). Differentiating, \(f_Y(y)=\frac1{2\sqrt y}\) on \((0,1]\) — a density that blows up at 0 yet integrates to 1. Then \(\mathbb E(Y)=\int_0^1\frac y{2\sqrt y}\,\mathrm dy=\int_0^1\frac{\sqrt y}2\,\mathrm dy=\frac13\), agreeing with \(\mathbb E(X^2)=\int_0^1x^2\,\mathrm dx=\frac13\). The cdf method of the template is the reliable route for any transformation.

Question 7STEP-style · Hard

Aurelia and Berenice take turns rolling a fair die, Aurelia first; the first to roll a six wins.
(i) Show that Aurelia wins with probability \(\frac6{11}\).
(ii) Find the expected number of rolls in the game.
(iii) Generalise: with success probability \(p\) per roll, show the first player's winning probability is \(\frac1{2-p}\), and comment on the limits \(p\to0\) and \(p\to1\).

Show solution

(i) Either sum the geometric series \(\frac16\left(1+\left(\frac56\right)^2+\left(\frac56\right)^4+\dots\right)=\frac{1/6}{1-25/36}=\frac6{11}\), or argue by self-similarity: if both miss, the game restarts, so \(a=\frac16+\left(\frac56\right)^2a\).
(ii) The number of rolls is geometric with \(p=\frac16\): expectation 6.
(iii) \(a=p+(1-p)^2a\) gives \(a=\frac p{1-(1-p)^2}=\frac1{2-p}\). As \(p\to1\), \(a\to1\) (the first roll settles it); as \(p\to0\), \(a\to\frac12\) — the first-mover advantage evaporates when success is rare, a one-line answer that would take pages by brute force.

Question 8New · Normal

The mass of a loaf of bread is modelled as \(X\sim N(800,15^2)\) grams. Find (i) \(\mathbb P(X>830)\), and (ii) the mass \(m\) such that 95% of loaves weigh more than \(m\). (Use \(\Phi(2)=0.9772\), \(\Phi(1.645)=0.95\).)

Show solution

(i) \(\mathbb P(X>830)=\mathbb P(Z>2)=1-0.9772=0.0228\).
(ii) "95% above \(m\)" means \(\mathbb P(X<m)=0.05\), so \(\frac{m-800}{15}=-1.645\): \(m=800-24.7\approx775\) g. The sign is the trap — sketch the bell and shade before computing.

Question 9New · Normal, inverse

A machine fills bottles with volume \(X\sim N(\mu,5^2)\) ml. Exactly 2.5% of bottles contain more than 510 ml. Find \(\mu\). (Use \(\Phi(1.96)=0.975\).)

Show solution

\(\mathbb P(X>510)=0.025\) means \(\frac{510-\mu}{5}=1.96\), so \(\mu=510-9.8=500.2\) ml. Inverse problems always start from the \(z\)-value, never from the probability directly.

Question 10New · Approximation

A fair coin is tossed 400 times. Using a suitable approximation, estimate the probability of at least 220 heads. (Use \(\Phi(1.95)=0.9744\).)

Show solution

\(X\sim B(400,\tfrac12)\approx N(200,10^2)\) since \(np=200\), \(np(1-p)=100\). With the continuity correction, \(X\ge220\) becomes \(X>219.5\): \[\mathbb P\!\left(Z>\tfrac{219.5-200}{10}\right)=\mathbb P(Z>1.95)=1-0.9744=0.0256.\]

Question 11New · Chi-squared

A genetics model predicts offspring types in the ratio \(9:3:3:1\). In 160 offspring the observed counts are \(95, 25, 28, 12\). Test the model at the 5% significance level. (Critical value: \(\chi^2_3(5\%)=7.815\).)

Show solution

Expected counts: \(160\times\frac9{16},\frac3{16},\frac3{16},\frac1{16}=90,30,30,10\) (all \(\ge5\) ✓). Then \[\chi^2=\frac{25}{90}+\frac{25}{30}+\frac4{30}+\frac4{10}\approx0.278+0.833+0.133+0.400=1.64.\] With \(\nu=4-1=3\): \(1.64<7.815\), so do not reject \(H_0\) — the data are consistent with the \(9{:}3{:}3{:}1\) model. (Consistent, not proved: see step 5 of the template.)

Question 12New · Contingency table

In a trial, 50 patients receive a treatment and 50 a placebo. Of the treated, 30 improve; of the placebo group, 20 improve. Test, at the 5% level, whether improvement is independent of treatment. (Critical value: \(\chi^2_1(5\%)=3.841\).)

Show solution

Totals: 50 improved, 50 did not, grand total 100 — so every expected count is \(\frac{50\times50}{100}=25\). Each observed cell (30, 20, 20, 30) misses its expectation by 5: \[\chi^2=4\times\frac{25}{25}=4.\] Degrees of freedom \((2-1)(2-1)=1\); since \(4>3.841\), reject independence at 5% — though only just, a reminder that "significant" is a threshold, not a chasm. (At the 1% level, critical value 6.635, the same data would not reject.)
